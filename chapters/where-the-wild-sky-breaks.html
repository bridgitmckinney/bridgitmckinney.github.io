Chapter OneThe Caldwell Son

The train came west through country that seemed determined to swallow every sign of civilization behind it. Caelon Harrington Caldwell stood beside the window of his private compartment with one shoulder against the polished walnut frame, watching towns diminish into settlements, settlements disappear into grassland, and eventually even the telegraph poles seem too insignificant for the country surrounding them. Six years in the East had accustomed him to Boston streets crowded with carriages, New York hotels filled with cigar smoke and ambition, private clubs where fortunes changed hands over whiskey, and drawing rooms where a man's prospects were measured before anyone bothered discovering whether he was interesting. Montana remained exactly as he remembered it—vast, severe, beautiful, and seemingly unconcerned by mankind's persistent attempts to impose order upon it.

He had left home at twenty-two and was returning at twenty-eight in the late summer of 1888, although sometimes those six years seemed far longer than that. Harvard had taught him political economy and mathematics, and enough theory about capital to understand why his father regarded academics with equal measures of usefulness and suspicion. The education Reginald Caldwell had purchased for him had continued well beyond lecture halls, because Caelon had spent vacations and later months moving through Boston and New York banking circles, studying commercial law, accounting, railroad financing, livestock markets and forward contracts, shipping contracts, insurance, and the elaborate ways men could legally steal from one another while calling the process commerce. He was returning to Montana with more than books in his trunks; he carried relationships with bankers, lawyers, investors, railroad men, and manufacturers whose names already mattered far beyond the East.

Some of those men considered him a friend, and more than a few owed him favors that Caelon had no intention of forgetting. Others would have gladly watched him lose every dollar attached to the Caldwell name, because he had learned early that making powerful friends almost inevitably meant acquiring powerful enemies beside them. He had embarrassed one banker by identifying a deliberately concealed weakness in a land investment before three dozen men, helped another financier force a rival out of a railroad syndicate, and once walked away from a proposed partnership after discovering the senior partner intended to use Caldwell cattle holdings as security for debts Caelon had never agreed to assume. Reginald had responded to that last story in a letter containing only seven words: At least Harvard did not make you stupid.

The memory brought a faint smile to Caelon's mouth. His father had never believed education could replace judgment, and Caelon had spent six years discovering that the old man was largely correct. A professor could explain how markets behaved when men acted rationally, but the most profitable men Caelon knew seemed determined to prove that greed, vanity, fear, lust, and spite controlled just as much money as reason ever had. Caelon had become good at recognizing all five, perhaps because he possessed enough of each to identify them comfortably in other people.

The East had educated him in other matters as well. Caelon had arrived in Boston young, handsome, wealthy, and sufficiently western to seem interesting without being uncivilized, a combination that had drawn female attention almost immediately. By the time he was twenty-eight, there had been enough beautiful women in his life that he had stopped keeping any meaningful count: wealthy widows bored with respectable men, unmarried daughters of industrialists testing rebellion before accepting suitable husbands, actresses who cared nothing for family names, and occasionally women whose husbands spent too many months elsewhere conducting business of their own. None had possessed his heart, and Caelon had never lied to himself by pretending otherwise.

Caelon enjoyed women enormously. He enjoyed their company, their laughter, their softness, their intelligence when they possessed it, their ambition when they admitted it, and the complicated private selves often hidden beneath the manners society required them to display. He also enjoyed their beds and their bodies, the warmth of a woman curled against him, the uncomplicated pleasure of mutual desire, and the temporary intimacy of nights that asked very little once morning came. He had learned to flirt without promising, leave without unnecessary cruelty, and recognize the dangerous moment when amusement began transforming into expectation, while more than a few women had wanted him simply because looking at Caelon Caldwell made otherwise sensible women curious about what removing his clothes might reveal.

He had never been falsely modest about his appearance. At six feet four inches, broad through the shoulders and narrow through the waist, Caelon had inherited his father's commanding frame but not his darker coloring. His hair was platinum blond, so pale that bright sunlight could turn it almost silver, while his eyes were an electric green vivid enough to startle people the first time they looked directly into them. Against skin browned by years beneath the Montana sun, the combination was striking enough that even in the East, where wealth could purchase almost every conceivable form of beauty, Caelon Caldwell had rarely entered a crowded room unnoticed.

His strong jaw and rugged features might have made him intimidating if his smile had not changed his entire face. That smile had disarmed stern matrons, encouraged shy daughters, irritated jealous husbands, and convinced more than one woman that perhaps the stories about western men had some merit after all. Six years of watching women lose their train of thought had taught him exactly what effect his appearance could have, and denying the evidence would have required an absurd kind of false modesty. Caelon did not spend his mornings admiring himself in mirrors, but neither did he pretend the mirror lied.

Beautiful women noticed him, ordinary women noticed him, married women noticed him, and occasionally men who disliked him seemed particularly offended by the ease with which their wives noticed him. Consequently, Caelon had developed the comfortable arrogance of a man rarely forced to wonder whether attraction was mutual. His mother called that arrogance one of his more exhausting characteristics, while his father regarded it as harmless provided Caelon never confused being desired with being respected. Reginald had told him at seventeen that a handsome face might convince someone to open a door but only an idiot expected it to keep the building standing, and Caelon had remembered the lesson without seeing any reason not to enjoy every door that opened willingly.

The train whistle sounded long and low, pulling him from the memories. Caelon looked ahead and saw the station coming into view, little more than timber, platform, dust, and scattered buildings beneath an enormous sky. His pulse changed before he consciously recognized why, and he found himself searching the platform through the glass. Several Blue Sky ranch hands waited beside a wagon intended for his luggage, but the man Caelon was looking for stood apart from them beneath a black hat.

Reginald Caelon Caldwell had apparently decided that waving remained beneath his dignity. At sixty-one, he still stood straight and broad, one hand resting over the carved silver head of a walking stick Caelon knew perfectly well he did not need. The years had placed gray through his dark hair and deepened the lines around his mouth, but little else about him seemed softer than when Caelon had left. Reginald watched the approaching train with the same expression he might have worn inspecting a thousand newly purchased cattle: interested, critical, and prepared to be disappointed if the merchandise failed expectations.

Caelon laughed quietly to himself as the train slowed. For six years he had received letters containing business instructions, criticism, market predictions, occasional insults, and exactly three direct admissions that his father missed him, all of which had appeared after enough whiskey that Reginald presumably regretted writing them the following morning. Caelon had kept every letter in a locked case beneath his clothes. He would have denied that fact loudly if anyone except Reginald ever discovered them.

The train settled beside the platform in a shriek of brakes and a rolling cloud of steam. Caelon stepped down before the porter could offer assistance, boots striking Montana boards for the first time in six years, and Reginald looked him over from expensive boots to tailored coat before studying his son's face. "You look expensive," Reginald said at last, and Caelon grinned before answering, "You paid for most of it." One corner of Reginald's mouth moved, which was as close to public delight as anyone standing nearby was likely to witness.

Reginald extended his hand, Caelon took it, and what began as a formal handshake became briefly something else when Reginald pulled him closer and struck one solid hand against his shoulder. It was not an embrace in any recognizable sense, but Caelon knew his father well enough to understand affection when Reginald attempted to conceal it beneath roughness. "Six years," Reginald said, and Caelon answered, "You counted." Reginald released him and replied, "I counted the bills from Harvard. I counted those particularly carefully."

Caelon laughed, and some internal tension he had carried since Massachusetts suddenly disappeared. The old bastard had not changed, and Caelon discovered with surprising force how relieved he was by that. Reginald turned toward the wagon while Caelon's trunks began coming off the train and said, "Your mother has spent the past week reorganizing the house because apparently you have forgotten what it looks like." He added that she had spent two hours moving flowers between tables the previous afternoon, and when he suggested she leave them where God originally put them, she had informed him that God possessed no understanding of drawing rooms.

Caelon fell into step beside him and asked how badly his mother had taken the suggestion. "Apparently God has no sense of interior arrangement," Reginald replied with complete seriousness, which only made Caelon laugh harder. One of the ranch hands approached for his leather case, and Caelon handed it over while greeting several men he recognized from boyhood. He was beginning to ask after another hand when something black beyond the platform caught his attention.

Caelon stopped. A massive stallion stood tied well away from the other horses, black from muzzle to tail except for one small pale marking concealed near a hind fetlock. Vader lifted his head the instant Caelon turned toward him, ears coming forward and powerful neck arching with sudden attention. Six years disappeared between one heartbeat and the next.

"You brought him," Caelon said, unable to keep the pleasure out of his voice. Reginald glanced toward the horse and replied, "I considered leaving the demon at home, but he has inflicted himself upon the rest of us for six years while waiting for you. It seemed only fair that you resume responsibility." Vader gave a deep snort as though he had understood every word and disagreed principally with the description. Caelon walked toward him without hesitation.

One of the younger ranch hands shifted instinctively before an older man caught his arm and muttered something Caelon could not hear. Vader stamped once, muscles rolling beneath his black coat, but the moment Caelon came within reach the stallion shoved his muzzle hard against Caelon's chest. Caelon rocked backward half a step and laughed with the uninhibited pleasure of a boy returning to something he had never stopped loving. He put both hands against Vader's face, pressed his forehead briefly against the horse's, and murmured, "You miserable bastard. Six years and you're still ugly."

Vader breathed across him and shoved harder, nearly knocking the expensive Harvard-educated heir to the Caldwell fortune into the dirt. Reginald watched with dry amusement and said, "No one has ridden him since you left." Caelon looked over his shoulder sharply and asked, "No one?" before his father replied, "Several attempted it. None attempted it twice."

Caelon stroked Vader's neck while Reginald explained that one man had broken his wrist, another had demolished part of a corral, and a particularly arrogant young hand had remained aboard for eleven seconds before landing in a water trough. "I told everyone before I left that nobody rides him," Caelon said. Reginald replied, "Young men interpret warnings as personal invitations to discover whether their bones are stronger than another man's." Caelon grinned and checked Vader's saddle himself before asking, "And?"

"Their bones were not." Reginald delivered the answer without expression. Caelon put his foot into the stirrup and swung into the saddle with the effortless familiarity of someone returning to his proper shape. Vader gathered beneath him instantly, every muscle alive, but the stallion made no attempt to resist. The difference was obvious enough that several of the younger hands continued staring.

Vader might tolerate being led by certain men, groomed by a few, and occasionally handled by the stablemaster when necessity demanded it, but Caelon remained the only human being permitted on his back. Caelon had never understood why the horse had chosen him so completely, and he had long ago stopped trying to explain it. The bond between them had formed years before Caelon left for the East and had apparently survived six years of separation intact. Some things, he supposed, had no need to be explained simply because other people found them unusual.

Reginald handed his walking stick to one of the men and mounted a tall chestnut gelding. "Your trunks can come with the wagon," he said. "Unless six years at Harvard have destroyed everything useful in you, I assume you still know the way home." Caelon settled deeper into Vader's saddle and answered, "I could find it blindfolded." Reginald replied, "Try that and I will leave you wherever he kills you."

Reginald nudged his horse forward before Caelon could answer, and Vader followed the instant the chestnut moved, the way a horse moves when it has finally been reunited with the only rider it will accept. Within moments father and son had left the station behind. Caelon did not look back. He had spent six years imagining this road home, and the reality felt both more familiar and stranger than memory had allowed.

The country opened around them in stages until Caelon felt as though he had been released from something he had not known was holding him. Boston had beauty, New York had energy, and there had been evenings in the East when wealth made the world glitter wonderfully, but neither city possessed this kind of space. Grass rolled beneath the wind toward distant ridges, cottonwoods gathered around wandering water, and mountains stood blue-gray against a sky so broad that architecture seemed ridiculous by comparison. Caelon drew a long breath filled with dust, horse, dry grass, and approaching autumn and thought, with absolute certainty, that he was home.

Reginald allowed him approximately five minutes of sentiment before returning to business. "What did Harvard teach you?" he asked, and Caelon glanced over before answering, "Do you want what the professors believed they taught me or what I actually learned?" Reginald looked faintly pleased and said, "The useful version." Caelon adjusted the reins while Vader selected his own path through uneven ground.

"Railroads are going to control cattle prices more than cattlemen are willing to admit, Eastern banks are extending too much credit against western land, refrigerated transport is going to keep changing where beef can profitably move, and anyone counting acreage instead of debt is probably lying to himself," Caelon said. He paused before adding that there was also more money looking west than there were competent men capable of managing what it bought. Reginald listened without interruption. Caelon recognized that silence immediately because his father used it during negotiations until other men became uncomfortable enough to weaken their own positions.

For nearly half a mile Reginald said nothing. Eventually he gave Caelon a single word: "Good." From Reginald Caldwell, that represented approximately the same enthusiasm other fathers might have expressed by throwing their sons a parade. Caelon laughed and asked, "Six years, and that's all I get?"

"What would you prefer?" Reginald asked. "A brass band?" Caelon suggested that a parade might be appropriate, and Reginald told him success had apparently damaged his judgment more than Harvard had. Caelon smiled because the single word had meant exactly what he knew it meant: Reginald was proud of him. There were few things in Caelon's life he valued more, though he would never have said so aloud.

They talked business for another mile. Caelon told him which Boston bank might provide better terms for expansion without demanding unacceptable control, which railroad representatives were worth speaking to, and which eastern investors should never be permitted close enough to Caldwell books to smell the ink. Reginald challenged every assumption, attacked several conclusions, and forced Caelon to defend each one until their conversation became less father and son than two businessmen testing whether the other had grown dull. Caelon enjoyed every minute of it.

Eventually Reginald asked about Benjamin Halworth, a Boston financier whose name Caelon had deliberately avoided mentioning. Caelon answered that Halworth would cheerfully shake a man's hand while purchasing the debt that would eventually take his house, and Reginald asked whether Caelon had offended him. Caelon admitted that telling Halworth this directly at dinner had perhaps been unnecessary. His father looked at him for several seconds before saying, "Good."

"That is twice you've praised me today," Caelon said, looking suspiciously toward him. "Are you dying?" Reginald responded by kicking his chestnut forward. Vader recognized the challenge before Caelon did and surged after him. The sudden race ended the conversation far more effectively than any answer could have.

They raced across the open ground like Caelon had never been gone. Reginald remained an exceptional rider for a man of sixty-one, but Vader was younger, faster, and deeply offended by the suggestion that another horse might remain ahead of him. Caelon leaned forward as the black stallion stretched into his stride, wind tearing at his coat and laughter escaping him without restraint. For several exhilarating minutes, Harvard, money, women, enemies, banks, railroads, and every expectation attached to the Caldwell name ceased to exist.

Vader won by several lengths. Reginald slowed his gelding beside them afterward and said, "Still showing off," while Caelon answered, "Still losing." Reginald gave him the particular glare that had reduced grown men to apologies during business meetings. It had never worked especially well on his son.

By late afternoon the Caldwell house appeared in the distance. It rose from the Montana landscape almost defiantly, an enormous structure of pale stone, dark timber, broad verandas, steep roofs, tall chimneys, and windows catching sunlight beneath the mountains. The family's money had existed before Reginald came fully into his own, but the empire surrounding the house belonged largely to him, built through cattle, land acquisition, calculated investment, ruthless bargaining, and the simple refusal to be ruined when lesser men had been. Caelon had grown up inside that house without understanding, until he left it, how extraordinary it truly was.

Now, approaching after six years, he understood something else about it. The place was enormous, beautiful, and built with every comfort money could bring west, but it was also large enough to make human beings disappear inside it. Caelon could count nights in Boston when ten people had occupied rooms smaller than the Caldwell entrance hall and somehow created more life than the entire house sometimes contained. Distance had changed the way he saw home without weakening the strange pull it still held over him.

His mother appeared on the veranda before they reached the yard. She wore a pale afternoon dress too refined for dust and stood beneath the carved porch as though expecting someone to paint the scene. Caelon dismounted and left Vader standing loose beside him because the horse would go nowhere unless Caelon did. His mother descended three steps before apparently deciding dignity mattered less than six years and hurried the remainder.

She embraced him tightly, and Caelon smiled against her hair as he returned it. He was genuinely pleased to see her despite knowing she would begin criticizing something about him within seconds, so when she pulled away and held him at arm's length, he silently began counting. Her eyes traveled immediately over his deeply tanned face, platinum hair, and broad shoulders before she announced, "You are too brown." Caelon grinned and said, "Four seconds."

His mother frowned and asked what he meant, while Reginald climbed the steps behind them and said, "I predicted six." Caelon kissed his mother's cheek before she could become offended. She inspected his coat, shoulders, hair, and face as though Harvard had been entrusted with one son and returned another without consulting her. "Your hair is almost white," she complained, and Caelon reminded her that it had always been nearly that pale.

"It was not that long," she said, reaching as if tempted to smooth his hair back into place with her fingers. She informed him that Boston apparently possessed no proper barbers and then remarked that his eyes looked even greener against the sun in his skin, which she seemed to consider another inconvenience. Caelon told her Boston possessed several thousand barbers and that he had personally offended every one of them. Reginald walked past and remarked that she had been given one heir and was apparently determined to remodel him.

The entrance hall looked almost exactly as Caelon remembered. The great staircase curved upward beneath a chandelier imported at absurd expense, bronze lamps stood along walls decorated with landscapes and family portraits, and polished wood reflected late afternoon light from windows tall enough to make ordinary houses seem embarrassed. Servants moved quietly through rooms designed to accommodate dozens of guests, yet with only three Caldwells occupying them the place seemed strangely hollow. Caelon had never noticed the echoes when he was younger.

Dinner was served in a dining room capable of seating twenty-four. Three people occupied it, leaving the polished mahogany table stretching between them like another piece of Caldwell territory. His mother talked enough for six absent guests, providing updates on neighbors, society, marriages, scandals, deaths, and every respectable family that had asked when Caelon would return. Reginald ate roast beef and responded only when necessary, displaying the practiced indifference of a husband who had spent decades allowing his wife authority over everything he considered socially irrelevant.

Reginald did not love her. Caelon had understood that long before leaving Montana, although neither parent had ever said the words aloud. His father did not mistreat her and provided anything she reasonably wanted, but the intimacy between them had died years before Caelon was old enough to understand what intimacy was. In Reginald's mind, his wife had given him an heir, preserved the household, maintained social appearances, and therefore fulfilled the obligations he considered important.

Eventually his mother set down her wineglass with suspicious deliberation. "There is someone you should meet," she said, and Caelon continued cutting his beef before replying, "That sentence cost me no end of trouble in Boston." His father coughed once into his fist, suspiciously close to laughter. His mother ignored both of them.

"Victoria Montgomery Sterling." She pronounced the name with satisfaction, as though Caelon should recognize immediately that his future had just been solved. "Her family is exceptionally well established, her father controls banking and railroad interests, she is beautifully educated, accomplished, remarkably elegant, and by every account one of the finest young women in New York society." Caelon looked at Reginald and said, "You knew."

Caelon said nothing else aloud, though the name had already landed somewhere his mother could not see. Victoria was William Sterling's elder daughter, and William Sterling's younger daughter was Elizabeth, a woman Caelon had taken to bed more times than he cared to count during his last two years in Boston, an arrangement his mother knew nothing about and one he intended to keep that way. Whatever genuine qualities Victoria possessed, courting the sister of a woman he had so recently and so thoroughly been involved with was not a complication he had the slightest interest in inviting into his life, and he saw no reason to explain any of that across his mother's dinner table. His father continued eating and replied, "Your mother has discussed Miss Sterling sufficiently often that men working in Wyoming probably know." Caelon's mother gave him a cool stare before turning back toward her son. "She would suit you," she said with enough certainty to make the matter sound already settled. Caelon lifted his glass and answered, "You have reached that conclusion without determining whether I suit her."

His mother smiled faintly. "I have seen women look at you, Caelon." She let her gaze settle briefly on his platinum hair and vivid green eyes as if his appearance itself were evidence for the prosecution. Six years in the East had produced enough proof to make false modesty ridiculous, and Caelon could hardly argue with her conclusion. He leaned back and asked what exactly she expected from this introduction.

"Marriage, eventually." Her answer came without hesitation, delivered with the same certainty she might have used to announce the weather. Caelon stopped smiling. Reginald finally put down his fork, watching his son's face with the same attention he usually reserved for cattle prices.

"The Sterling match makes sense," Reginald said. There was no romance in his tone because he approached marriage with the same practicality he brought to breeding stock, railroad access, and financing. "Their fortune is sound, their connections are useful, the woman appears intelligent, and your mother assures me she is beautiful enough that you will not spend your marriage looking elsewhere during breakfast." Caelon's mother stared at him in outrage.

"Reginald." Her voice carried warning, but he ignored it, cutting another piece of roast beef with the unhurried calm of a man who had weathered worse looks across this table. Caelon smiled despite himself and said, "A stirring endorsement." His father replied without glancing up, "I am not writing poetry. You need a wife eventually, and you need children."

"There it is." Caelon lifted his wine. "My true purpose." Reginald's gaze settled on him with uncomfortable accuracy before he answered. Caelon already knew he was not going to like whatever came next.

"Your purpose is to ensure everything I spent my life building does not end because my only son becomes too entertained by women to produce an heir." Caelon briefly wondered how much information traveled from Boston back to Montana and decided he preferred not knowing. His mother looked between them as though both men were conspiring to destroy everything graceful about marriage. Reginald appeared completely untroubled by her disapproval.

Caelon had no objection to marriage in principle. He simply had never met a woman who made permanence seem preferable to freedom, and he saw no compelling reason to manufacture affection because two fortunes looked attractive beside one another on paper. He had known extraordinary women in the East, including some beautiful enough to turn entire rooms when they entered, and had desired several with real enthusiasm. None had ever made him imagine waking beside the same face twenty years later.

"No," Caelon said. His mother's expectant expression remained in place for several seconds before she seemed to realize he had actually refused her, while Reginald's gaze lifted with far greater interest than the conversation had previously earned. Caelon took another drink of wine as though he had declined nothing more important than dessert. He had spent six years dealing with men who believed persistence could transform refusal into negotiation, and he had no intention of allowing that lesson to follow him into his mother's dining room.

His mother stared at him. "No, what?" she asked, and Caelon almost smiled because there were very few circumstances in which the meaning of the word required clarification. "No, I will not meet Victoria Montgomery Sterling for the purpose of allowing the two of you to inspect whether we might produce satisfactory Caldwell grandchildren," he said. "If I ever marry, I will choose the woman myself."

"You have not even met her," his mother replied, and the calm in her voice acquired the brittle quality Caelon remembered from childhood. She began listing Victoria's virtues again—beauty, education, breeding, wealth, family connections—as though additional evidence might repair the fundamental problem. Caelon listened until she finished and then said, "She sounds magnificent." His mother relaxed slightly until he added, "She may marry anyone she pleases."

Reginald's mouth shifted almost imperceptibly, though whether from amusement or irritation Caelon could not tell. "You need an heir," he said, and Caelon answered, "Eventually." Reginald reminded him that eventually had a habit of arriving whether a man prepared for it or not, while Caelon pointed out that he was twenty-eight rather than eighty. Neither man raised his voice, which made the disagreement feel more serious rather than less.

His mother leaned forward. "You are behaving as though I suggested some dreadful woman simply because her father has money." Caelon shook his head and replied that Victoria's qualities were irrelevant because he had no intention of courting a woman selected before he had even returned home. "You arranged the entire matter without asking me," he said. "Now you are asking me to participate after the decision has already been made."

"No decision has been made," she insisted. Caelon looked directly at her and said, "Good. Then there is nothing to discuss." His mother's expression hardened, and Reginald finally placed his napkin beside his plate. The gesture was small, but both of them knew it meant he had decided to enter the argument rather than merely observe it.

"You will marry someday," Reginald said. Caelon met his father's eyes and answered, "Probably." Reginald continued, "You will have children," and Caelon replied, "I expect to enjoy arranging that part far more than you apparently expect." His mother's scandalized look nearly made him laugh, but Reginald's expression did not change.

"You find this amusing now," Reginald said. "You will find it a good deal less amusing when the future of everything outside these windows depends upon what happens after you are dead." Caelon glanced toward the dark glass and then back at his father. "Then I suppose I had better avoid dying before I find a woman I actually want."

Reginald studied him for several long seconds. "Stubbornness is useful until a man begins mistaking it for judgment." Caelon leaned back in his chair and answered, "You taught me that judgment means making my own decisions." His father's eyes narrowed slightly. "I also taught you to recognize a good arrangement when someone puts one in front of you."

"And I recognize one," Caelon said. "I simply do not want it." His mother opened her mouth again, but Reginald raised one hand and stopped her without looking away from his son. Something passed silently between the two men then: challenge, affection, irritation, and the mutual certainty that neither was going to surrender tonight.

Later, after the house had fallen quiet, Caelon went outside rather than retire. Six years of cities had taught him how much he disliked silence indoors and how deeply he missed it outside, where quiet never felt empty because wind, insects, animals, and distant water occupied it. Vader stood in the nearest paddock alone, the other horses having long ago learned the wisdom of giving him a wide berth. The black stallion approached the fence when Caelon came near and lowered his head within reach.

Caelon scratched beneath Vader's jaw and said, "Apparently my mother intends to marry me." Vader regarded him without enthusiasm, so Caelon added, "Victoria Montgomery Sterling," as though the horse might recognize the name from society pages. Vader snorted and turned his head away. Caelon laughed softly and said, "Precisely."

He leaned against the fence and looked beyond the horse toward moonlit grasslands stretching so far that darkness eventually swallowed them. Somewhere across those thousands of acres cattle slept, streams moved through cottonwoods, wild animals crossed fences without permission, and men hired by his father guarded what every legal document in Montana recognized as Blue Sky property. Caelon understood the world in those terms so completely that he had never considered there might be another way to understand it. The very idea that ownership itself could be questioned would have sounded to him like questioning whether winter was cold.

The ranch was theirs. The cattle were theirs, the buildings were theirs, the water rights were theirs, Reginald's chestnut horse was his property, and Vader belonged to Caelon with an exclusivity the stallion himself seemed entirely willing to enforce. One day all of it would pass from Reginald Caelon Caldwell to Caelon Harrington Caldwell, and Caelon had spent six years preparing himself to receive that responsibility. He had returned from Harvard believing that future was not merely inevitable but desirable.

Inside the house, his mother was almost certainly deciding that refusal was merely an obstacle requiring a better strategy. His father would be thinking about heirs, inheritance, and how long he was prepared to tolerate his son's resistance before interfering. Caelon knew both of them too well to imagine the conversation had ended simply because dinner had. He looked across the dark sweep of ranchland, smiled to himself, and decided they were going to be disappointed.

He had spent six years learning how to prevent powerful men from deciding his future for him. Caelon Caldwell had no intention of coming home and allowing his mother to succeed where bankers, investors, rivals, and ambitious women had failed. Whatever woman eventually became his wife would be there because he wanted her, not because two wealthy families had discovered their fortunes looked attractive beside one another. On that point, he was certain there was nothing left to negotiate.

Chapter TwoThe Work of It

By the third week after Caelon's return, the ranch had stopped treating him like a guest. The trunks from Boston were unpacked, his eastern coats hung upstairs beside clothes better suited to dust and sweat, and the servants no longer looked surprised when he came through the kitchen before sunrise asking for coffee strong enough to strip paint. Reginald had eased himself out of most of the daily field work without announcing the change, keeping the ledgers, contracts, payroll, railroad correspondence, purchasing, land matters, and negotiations in his own hands while Caelon took charge of nearly everything beyond the study windows. Anything that breathed, kicked, broke, wandered, flooded, froze, or required a man on horseback now had a habit of finding its way to him.

Caelon preferred it that way. There was satisfaction in business, and he enjoyed beating another man across a table as much as Reginald did, but paper never lied quite as honestly as livestock. A steer either carried weight or it did not, a fence either held or failed, a horse either moved soundly or favored a leg, and no polished argument could persuade a dry creek bed to produce water. Before the sun cleared the eastern ridges most mornings, Caelon had already been through the main stable aisle, spoken with the night hand, and decided which problem deserved attention first.

The Blue Sky stables stood beyond the main house across a wide yard packed hard by hooves, wagon wheels, and years of work. Heavy timber framed the long building, stone footings protected the lower walls from rot and snow, and a steep roof rose high enough to shed a Montana winter without complaint. Valuable horses occupied the box stalls nearest the tack room, while most working mounts rotated between pasture and stable according to weather, injury, and need. Saddles, bridles, blankets, medicines, grain bins, farrier tools, grooming equipment, and enough leather to outfit a cavalry troop filled the adjoining rooms, and within a week Caelon knew exactly which men returned things where they belonged.

That morning he found a saddle on the wrong rack before he found the man responsible. Nineteen-year-old Lewis Harper stared at it as though hoping the leather might confess to moving itself, while Caelon stood nearby with his coffee. Harper was eager, quick with horses, and cursed with the conviction that speed excused whatever mess he left behind. Caelon set his cup on the shelf and asked, "Whose saddle?"

"Mine, sir." Harper glanced toward the doorway, then back at Caelon, already aware that escape had become unlikely. "I came in late after checking the north pasture and meant to put it right before breakfast." He reached for the saddle, but Caelon turned the skirt back first.

Dried sweat had crusted along the underside, and one edge of the leather had begun to stiffen. Caelon ran his thumb over it, then looked at Harper without raising his voice. "Your horse carries you all day, and this is what you put on his back tomorrow?" Harper's ears reddened before he answered, and the hand he had extended toward the saddle dropped to his side.

"No, sir." Harper reached for the saddle again, and this time Caelon let him take it. "Clean it, oil the dry places, check the latigo, and put it where it belongs," Caelon said. "You can be tired after the horse is cared for."

Harper nodded and carried the saddle toward the cleaning bench. Caelon picked up his coffee and continued down the aisle without another word. A man who learned from correction was worth keeping, and a man who needed humiliation before he listened usually created a different kind of trouble. Harper had not yet given him reason to decide which sort he would become.

Vader occupied the largest stall at the far end. The black stallion raised his head before Caelon reached him, ears coming forward as though three weeks of reunion had erased six years of separation. Caelon opened the stall, checked both forelegs, lifted each hoof, and ran his hands along the animal's shoulder and girth line before examining a faint rub left by the previous day's saddle. Vader endured the inspection until Caelon bent near his flank, then twisted his neck and attempted to catch a loose curl of platinum hair between his teeth.

"Eat your breakfast." Caelon pushed the muzzle away and straightened, only to have Vader return it to his shoulder. "You have grain in front of you and still choose to irritate me." The stallion exhaled warm breath against his neck and remained exactly where he was.

Across the aisle, Elias Boone watched without pretending otherwise. Boone had worked for Reginald nearly twenty years and carried those years in a face browned and creased by weather, with pale eyes that missed very little and a manner that wasted even less. He trusted horses before men, had argued against three of Reginald's purchases strongly enough to be proven right twice, and possessed the rare privilege of disagreeing with either Caldwell without becoming nervous first. Caelon had liked him since childhood.

"South fence is down again," Boone said. He waited until Caelon stepped out of Vader's stall before adding, "Cottonwood Wash. Thompson saw six cows through before dark and got them back." Caelon asked how much fence they had lost, and Boone shrugged one shoulder before answering, "Maybe eighty yards. Could be more."

The storm two nights earlier had driven enough water and debris through the wash to make any estimate suspect. Caelon asked whether anyone had checked the posts beyond the obvious break, and Boone shook his head. "Send Martinez, Bell, and Harper with a wagon, staples, wire, two post mauls, and enough cedar posts to replace twenty," Caelon said. "If a post is soft at the base or split through, pull it and replace it. I don't want them fixing the same stretch again next week."

Boone gave a short nod and turned to carry out the order. Before he left, he asked whether Caelon intended to meet the men at the wash. Caelon reached for Vader's saddle and said he would join them after moving the western herd. Boone glanced toward Harper, who was scrubbing his saddle with renewed devotion, and said, "I'll tell the boy breakfast can wait."

The western herd had already begun bunching near the lower basin when Caelon reached the gate. Six riders waited along the edge, their horses shifting beneath them while cattle moved across the grass in a slow churn of backs, horns, calves, dust, and bawling. The work was simple when reduced to words: move the herd north onto fresh grazing, keep cows and calves together, stop the younger steers from breaking toward the creek, and avoid running weight off animals meant for market. Nothing involving several hundred cattle remained simple for very long.

Caelon rode the line before giving the signal to move. He spotted three calves trapped behind a stand of willow, sent one rider east where the ground narrowed between the fence and a shallow ravine, and had another hold back while the lead cows found the direction he wanted. Satisfied with the positions, he lifted one arm and gave the signal for the herd to begin moving. The herd began moving reluctantly. Lead cows drifted north, calves called and pushed toward familiar mothers, and several young steers tested the edges before deciding the riders were more persistent than they were. Dust lifted under hundreds of hooves and settled over the basin until horses and men wore the same pale film. Caelon kept Vader moving along the western side, turning one animal with position and another with pressure, rarely using speed where patience did more.

A red steer broke hard toward the creek halfway through the move. One of the younger riders chased too quickly, and the animal lengthened its stride instead of turning. Caelon cut across the slope, Vader gathering beneath him at the change in direction, and reached the steer's shoulder before it found open ground. He pressed just enough to bend the animal back toward the herd, then eased away.

"Don't chase what you can turn," Caelon called. The younger hand lifted an arm in acknowledgment and dropped back into place. Caelon offered nothing more because the steer had already made the point. They kept moving north.

Three miles took most of the morning. The pace stayed slow because of the calves, and the first cattle were spreading over fresh grass by the time the sun stood well clear of the mountains. Caelon remained after the gate closed, watching the herd settle rather than assuming the work ended because the animals had reached the correct pasture. He rode through twice looking for stragglers, injuries, and anything in the way a cow moved that suggested trouble.

A yearling heifer favored her right hind leg near the water. Caelon dismounted, separated her with help from one of the hands, and found a torn strip of hide just above the hoof where wire had probably caught her. Dirt had worked into the wound, though it had not gone deep. He cleaned it, wrapped the leg, and ordered her moved to the closer holding pasture where she could be checked again that evening.

"You planning to doctor every cow yourself?" Tom Rourke asked. The older hand sat his horse nearby with one thumb hooked beneath his belt, amusement in his voice rather than criticism. Caelon stood and wiped his hands on a cloth before watching the heifer limp away between two riders. "Only the ones polite enough to injure themselves in front of me."

Rourke chuckled. "Your father would've told somebody else to do it." Caelon looked toward the northern pasture, where the herd had begun spreading instead of bunching along the gate. "My father already did his years of this." Rourke studied him for a moment and nodded, then turned his horse toward the others.

By the time Caelon reached Cottonwood Wash, Boone's estimate had proved generous. A fallen cottonwood had torn wire and posts down the bank, floodwater had loosened several more, and another stretch farther south sagged badly enough that one hard push from a cow would open it. Martinez and Bell were cutting branches while Harper drove the first replacement post. Caelon dismounted, took off his coat, and walked the damaged line before touching anything.

Two posts farther along were split nearly to the ground, and another gave beneath his boot when he tested the base. "Pull these three too," he said. Martinez sighed at the extra work, though there was no real complaint in it. Caelon picked up the second post maul and stepped into place beside them.

They worked through the afternoon. Caelon drove cedar until sweat soaked through his shirt and his shoulders burned, then moved along the line checking depth, alignment, wire tension, and the condition of every post they had chosen to keep. His platinum hair had been tied loosely at the nape when they started, but curls escaped as the hours passed and stuck damply against his neck. Dust settled over his boots, forearms, and face until little remained of the Harvard-educated heir his mother had welcomed home.

Harper drove one post crooked and froze with both hands around the maul. He looked at Caelon, then at the post, already knowing what was coming. Caelon said nothing. Harper closed his eyes, muttered beneath his breath, and began pulling the post back out.

Martinez laughed. "He's learning." Caelon held the replacement steady while Harper set it again. "He's learning I can see." Harper muttered something louder, and the men laughed hard enough that even Caelon smiled.

They finished near sunset. Caelon rode another quarter mile beyond the repaired section before turning home, checking wire and posts as he went, satisfied that the line would hold through the next storm at least. He turned Vader back toward the house as the light began to fail over the cottonwoods along the river. Caelon reached the house after the dinner bell had rung. A stable hand took Vader only after Caelon loosened the girth himself and checked the rub beneath it, then Caelon crossed the yard with his coat over one shoulder and dirt streaked through his clothes. His mother met him in the entrance hall, took one look at his boots, and stopped. Her gaze traveled down the dried mud along his trousers and returned to his face.

"You are late." She did not raise her voice; she never needed to when displeasure could be communicated more elegantly. Caelon glanced toward the dining room and caught the smell of roast beef, fresh bread, and rosemary. "The fence lacked respect for your schedule." His mother moved aside before his boots came close to the carpet.

"It also appears to have attacked your clothing." She looked at his shirt, then the dirt across one sleeve. "Change. We have waited long enough." Caelon kissed her cheek in passing and headed upstairs before she could inspect him more closely.

No one mentioned Cottonwood Wash over dinner. Reginald asked whether Caelon wanted more wine, his mother discussed a letter from an acquaintance in New York, and the only subject remotely resembling business appeared when she found a way to mention Victoria Montgomery Sterling for the fourth time that week. She described an autumn musicale Victoria had attended, the Sterling house in Newport, and a photograph she had written east to request without asking Caelon whether he wanted one. Caelon continued eating and made no effort to disguise his lack of interest.

"I have already said no," he reminded her. His mother rested two fingers against the stem of her wineglass and informed him that refusing to meet a woman he had never seen was stubbornness masquerading as judgment. Reginald continued eating, making no attempt to rescue either of them. Caelon looked across the table and caught the faintest amusement in his father's eyes.

"You are enjoying this," Caelon said. Reginald swallowed before answering, "I enjoy roast beef." His wife gave him a look sharp enough to cut through the remainder of the meal. Caelon returned to his plate before the smile at the corner of his mouth betrayed him.

Dinner ended without one word about freight, cattle weights, payroll, fences, banks, or pasture. That boundary belonged to Reginald and had existed long before Caelon went east: the dining room was for family, guests, conversation, and his wife's persistent campaign to secure descendants. Business belonged elsewhere. Even Reginald, who could turn almost any hour of the day toward money, kept that distinction intact.

Caelon left the dining room after dinner and went downstairs knowing where his father expected him. The study doors stood open, lamplight falling across the hall from a room lined with books, maps, correspondence cabinets, and shelves of ledgers dating back years. Reginald sat behind the broad desk with his coat removed, his collar loosened, and a cigar burning low in one hand. He did not look up when Caelon entered.

"Close the door." Reginald finished the line he was writing before setting down the pen. Caelon shut the door and crossed toward the chair opposite the desk, where a second glass and a measure of whiskey already waited. The ritual had developed during his first week home and had not been discussed since.

Every night ended there. Caelon brought the ranch inside with him—the cattle, water, fences, horses, grazing, labor, weather, repairs, and whatever had gone wrong before breakfast—while Reginald opened the other half of the empire. Contracts, freight rates, payroll, bank letters, taxes, land disputes, supply orders, legal correspondence, market prices, and negotiations passed between them until Caelon knew as much about what happened beyond the ranch as he did about what happened upon it. Reginald had stepped away from much of the physical work, but not from preparing Caelon to understand the whole of what he would inherit.

"How bad was Cottonwood?" Reginald asked. Caelon sat down and stretched one aching shoulder before answering, feeling the day's work settle into his muscles now that he had finally stopped moving. "Worse than Boone thought. Cottonwood came down through the wire, six bad posts, three more I pulled because they wouldn't have lasted through winter." Reginald made no comment on the number, only reached for his pen and noted something small in the book at his elbow before waiting for the rest.

"Cattle through?" Reginald asked, when Caelon offered nothing further. It was the question that mattered most to him, and Caelon had learned early not to bury it beneath smaller details. "Six before Thompson found it. All recovered." Caelon picked up the whiskey but did not drink yet. "Western herd is north. One yearling with a wire cut, nothing deep."

Reginald nodded once and opened a small notebook without writing. He never needed Caelon to turn the day into a formal report; questions came where he wanted detail, and silence meant move on. Caelon told him about the red steer at the creek, Harper's neglected saddle, and the gate inspection he wanted done on the eastern section the following morning. Reginald pushed a sheet across the desk. "Now mine." Caelon took it and found revised freight rates from a railroad representative in Helena. The increase took effect after November and had been wrapped in enough polite language to make the numbers more irritating, not less. He read the page twice before looking up.

"They think we have no alternative." Caelon tapped the rate with one finger. Reginald drew on his cigar, the ember brightening in the dim room. "Do we?" Caelon looked back down.

"Not one they believe." He studied the lower figures. "Which matters more than whether we actually have one." Reginald's mouth shifted slightly.

"Go on." Caelon leaned back, already seeing the negotiation rather than the paper. "If we argue from necessity, they know we are trapped. If we start asking about volume guarantees and alternate shipping dates, they have to wonder what else we are considering."

"There is nothing else." Reginald watched him steadily. Caelon lifted the paper. "They do not know that." Reginald reached for the next letter without comment.

By the time the study clock struck ten, the day's fence line had given way to collateral language, rail schedules, cattle prices, and winter feed costs. Reginald tested Caelon's reasoning whenever it softened and let silence do the rest. Neither man treated the physical ranch and its books as separate businesses. One always reached into the other.

The next morning put that truth to work before breakfast. A hand arrived at the kitchen door reporting twelve horses had gone through a gate during the night after one hinge tore free. Two were valuable young geldings not yet fully trained, and one was a mare close enough to foaling that Caelon did not want her run hard. He finished half a cup of coffee, wrapped bread and cold meat in a cloth, and was mounted before sunlight broke across the yard.

The horses had scattered across the eastern meadow without going far. Caelon found tracks within twenty minutes and divided the riders so no one drove the loose animals faster than necessary. The mare returned easily once separated from the younger horses, but one gelding crossed a shallow stream and headed toward broken ground. The nearest hand started after him until Caelon lifted one arm.

"Leave him." The rider checked his horse and watched the gelding continue another hundred yards. Once pursuit disappeared, the animal slowed, looked back, and lowered his head toward the grass. Caelon waited before sending three riders wide around him.

They closed the distance at a walk, leaving enough space that the gelding never felt trapped. When he finally lifted his head, he considered another run but found no one chasing him into one. Within minutes he had been turned toward the other horses without sweat or panic. Caelon watched him settle among them before riding back toward the broken gate.

The hinge had not failed from wear. The bolts sat wrong, one board had split around the fitting, and somebody had hidden the weakness beneath a fresh length of timber after an earlier repair. Caelon ran his hand over the break and called for the section foreman. By noon he had ordered every gate on the eastern range checked.

Boone was waiting when Caelon reached the stable. A gray gelding had swelling beneath the saddle, two saddles needed new rigging, and one of the younger mares had begun striking the wall whenever another horse passed her stall. Caelon listened, took the gray out of work, sent the saddles to the leather bench, and went to see the mare himself. She stood rigid at the back of the stall with one ear pinned toward the aisle.

He spent twenty minutes with her. There was no trick to it and no audience worth impressing, only repetition, distance, pressure released at the right moment, and enough patience for the mare to stop expecting every movement outside the stall to become a threat. Boone watched from the doorway until the animal lowered her head and began chewing. "You planning on eating today?"

"Eventually." Caelon kept one hand against the mare's neck. Boone glanced toward the open door, where the sun had already climbed. "That's what your father says about sleeping." Caelon smiled.

"And yet he's alive." Boone pushed away from the frame. "For now." He walked off before Caelon could answer, already calling for fresh bedding.

Weeks gathered into a rhythm. Caelon rose before dawn, worked cattle, inspected fences, directed crews, ran the stables, dealt with injured stock, checked grazing, and came home with the day written across his clothes. At night, he sat opposite Reginald and learned what had happened beyond the ranch while explaining what had happened on it. Somewhere between those two halves, he began learning how to run all of what would one day be his.

His mother regarded this as a temporary disorder she expected civilization to correct. She had imagined Harvard returning a gentleman who would supervise through reports, dine at predictable hours, receive suitable company, and eventually become interested enough in matrimony to justify the list of women she had been keeping in her head for years. Instead, Caelon sometimes appeared at luncheon with wire cutters in one pocket and a broken spur in the other. He left the house looking like an heir and came home through the kitchen looking as though the ranch had attempted to keep him.

One evening she caught him crossing the entrance hall while pulling off his gloves. A shallow cut across the back of his hand had reopened, leaving a thin line of blood near his wrist. She looked at the marble before looking back at him. "You are bleeding on the floor."

"Very little." Caelon inspected the cut as though the quantity might support his defense. His mother's expression did not change. "That is not a defense."

"It is from where I'm standing." He kissed her cheek before she could begin and moved toward the stairs. Behind him, Reginald's voice came from the library doorway. "She had that floor brought from New York."

Reginald stood with one shoulder against the frame, looking at the blood with no sympathy for either the floor or his wife. Caelon glanced down and said, "Then it has traveled farther than most people. A little blood should broaden its character." His mother made a sound that required no translation and walked away. Reginald waited until she disappeared before the corner of his mouth moved.

"Your education was wasted." Caelon started upstairs. "Completely." Reginald returned to the library without bothering to disagree. Dinner followed an hour later, and neither man mentioned the cut again.

At dinner, Victoria Montgomery Sterling remained as persistent a presence as ever. Caelon's mother had received the requested photograph and placed it near her plate one evening with the air of someone introducing evidence before a jury. Victoria possessed the kind of face artists liked: poised, symmetrical, pale, and composed, with dark hair arranged flawlessly and eyes that looked unimpressed by the existence of the photographer. Caelon studied the image long enough to satisfy courtesy and set it back down.

"Well?" his mother asked. Caelon reached for his wine. "She has a face." Reginald lowered his fork and stared at his plate.

His mother's eyes narrowed. "A remarkably lovely one." Caelon nodded. "Then she has been fortunate." Reginald coughed into his napkin.

"I am surrounded by children." She retrieved the photograph and set it safely beyond Caelon's reach as if he might damage it through indifference. "Victoria is accomplished, intelligent, well educated, and accustomed to the responsibilities required of her position." Caelon cut into his dinner and said nothing.

"You could at least write to her." His mother watched him closely. Caelon looked up. "That would imply interest."

"It would imply manners," his mother said, setting the word down like a card she expected to win the hand. "It would imply exactly what you intend it to imply," Caelon replied, unmoved. He returned his attention to his plate, cutting another piece of beef with the same unhurried patience he brought to everything he had already decided. "No."

Reginald stayed out of it until his wife turned toward him. "You might say something." Reginald considered the request, took a drink, and looked at his son. "Your mother wants you to write to Victoria."

"I gathered that." Reginald nodded. "There. I said something." Caelon's mother shut her eyes briefly. The matter ended for that evening because continuing it would have required choosing which Caldwell man to strangle first.

After dinner, Caelon followed Reginald into the study. No mention of Victoria crossed the threshold with them. Reginald opened the evening with payroll, followed it with a supply contract, and then handed Caelon a letter from a Helena attorney concerning a dispute over water access near the western boundary. The photograph might as well have ceased to exist.

That was the shape of their life together now. Dinner belonged to family, however irritating family occasionally became. The study belonged to work. Neither man confused the two.

One night, Reginald opened a ledger after Caelon had finished reporting on the western herd. "Weights are better than last year." He turned the book and pushed it across the desk. Caelon recognized the figures collected over the previous month and saw the improvement immediately.

"The north grass held better." Caelon ran his finger down the column. "And we moved them slower." Reginald watched him for a moment before saying, "You changed the rotation." Caelon looked up.

"The south pasture was getting hit too hard," Caelon said, half expecting an argument. "I know," Reginald said, without looking up from the desk. Caelon's finger stopped on the page, and the surprise on his face was plain enough that he made no effort to hide it. "You knew?" Reginald folded his hands and waited, in no hurry to make the explanation easy.

"I knew the old rotation was losing efficiency," Reginald said. Caelon leaned back. "And you let me spend three weeks thinking I had found something you missed." His father's expression remained unreadable.

"I did miss something." Reginald glanced toward the ledger. "I did not know what you would replace it with." The distinction settled between them. Caelon looked back at the figures.

Part of him wanted to be annoyed. The numbers changed the shape of what his father had done before the feeling could take hold. Reginald had not stepped away because he was tired of the ranch; he had moved far enough aside to see whether Caelon could carry it without being led through every decision. Caelon closed the ledger.

"You could have told me," Caelon said, though there was more amusement in it than real complaint. Reginald reached for his cigar without any hurry at all. "I just did," he said. Caelon shook his head, recognizing the old maneuver for exactly what it was.

"You are an infuriating man," Caelon said, and meant it as something closer to admiration than accusation. "So I've been told," Reginald replied, entirely unbothered, drawing on the cigar until the ember caught fresh light. He smoked in silence for a moment before looking toward the ledger again, as though deciding whether Caelon had earned what came next. "You're good at it." The words came without ceremony, the way everything that mattered most to Reginald tended to arrive. Caelon went still.

Praise from his father remained rare enough to land harder than criticism ever did. Reginald did not repeat himself and reached for the next paper. Caelon lowered his eyes to the whiskey to hide the smile threatening anyway. "Careful. That's three compliments since I came home."

"Then I've become soft." Reginald opened the letter. Caelon settled deeper into the chair. "Tragic."

The study clock struck eleven before they finished. Caelon had two miles of fence inspection waiting for morning, three young horses needing work, and a hay inventory he did not trust because one count had been made by a man who considered estimation a legitimate mathematical method. Reginald had another railroad letter and a banker in Boston demanding revised security language. Both men stood only when there was nothing left worth discussing.

Caelon paused at the door. Reginald had already opened another book despite declaring the evening finished. "Go to bed," Caelon said. His father looked up with mild offense.

"I was planning to." Reginald glanced toward the open book. Caelon raised an eyebrow. "Eventually?"

Reginald stared at him for a beat before his mouth twitched. "Get out." Caelon laughed and closed the study door behind him. The lamp remained burning beneath it as he walked away.

The house had gone quiet. Beyond the windows, lanterns burned near the stable doors, cattle shifted somewhere across the dark fields, and a horse called once from a distant paddock before another answered. Caelon stopped near the front window and looked out at the darkened yard for a moment before turning away. His hands hurt, one shoulder ached, and tomorrow would begin before the sky brightened. There would be cattle to move, horses to inspect, men waiting for orders, and whatever fresh problem the ranch had prepared overnight. Caelon rubbed the back of his neck once, then went upstairs.
