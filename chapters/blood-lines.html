Chapter OneThe First Night

The first time it happened, no one knew. That was the thing about Alex and Andrew in those earliest weeks. They were quiet. They slept when they were supposed to sleep, ate when they were meant to eat, and watched the world from their cradles with a steadiness that made Venus check on them more often than sleeplessness required, not because anything appeared wrong but because nothing appeared wrong in a way she did not entirely trust. Ares had taken to standing over the cradles in the dark hours for the same reason, though neither of them had said that aloud to the other.

They were beautiful. Golden-haired and small, and in every visible way entirely ordinary, except for their eyes. Their eyes were blue. Not the blue of sky or water or anything so available as that. Something older than those things, a blue that had existed before the words to describe it. It did not belong to Ares. It did not belong to Venus. They had looked at each other over those cradles on the first morning and the silence between them had held the shape of a name neither of them said, and they had not said it since, and the blue eyes watched the world from the nursery and gave nothing away.

Ares appeared in the nursery most nights without examining the habit. He simply found himself there, moving past the door he had taken to leaving open, standing with his arms folded until he was satisfied. It was not strategy. He had no framework for this vigilance, which was part of what made it uncomfortable. He was a man who had always known what to do with a threat. These were his sons, sleeping. There was no threat. He checked on them anyway.

Venus found him there one night near the second hour. She stood in the doorway and watched him for a moment before she spoke, taking in the folded arms and the bare feet. "You checked twenty minutes ago."

He did not turn around. "A lot can happen in twenty minutes." He said it the way he reported troop movements, as if the number had been measured and found to be sufficient cause. His eyes did not leave the cradles.

"Name one thing," Venus said. She came no further into the room yet. "One thing that has happened in this nursery in the last twenty minutes."

"Andrew moved." Ares nodded at the far cradle without looking away from it. "I was standing right here, watching him, and I still did not see him do it."

She crossed the room and stood beside him. Together they looked down at the twins. Alex slept on his back with one fist curled beneath his chin. Andrew had migrated to the far edge of his blanket by some method neither of them had managed to witness, despite the fact that Ares had spent the better part of a week positioning himself to see both cradles from the doorway and had still not caught it happening. He had made his peace with that.

The quiet of the fortress in these weeks felt like something that could be revoked without warning. Venus had stopped saying so aloud. Ares had never said it, but she knew it was there in the same way she knew the sword was always within arm's reach and the door was always left open and he always checked, and checked again, not because something was wrong but because he had learned in two thousand years what it cost to assume that nothing was.

"They are going to be fine," Venus said. She said it softly, to the cradles as much as to him, as if the boys might hear it and take it as an order. It was what she had been telling herself every night for a week.

"Yes," Ares said. He did not move. He stood with his arms folded and his eyes on his sons, a god who had believed a great many things in his life without needing to stand over them all night to be sure.

She took his hand and guided him back to bed. He went, the way he went with the things he was reluctant to admit he was reluctant about, without argument and without speed, and behind them the fortress settled into its usual silence and the lamps burned lower and in the nursery both boys lay still. Asleep. Breathing. Exactly as they had been left. For a while.

Alex opened one eye. The room was dark, the only sounds the distant shift of the fortress walls against the cold and the faint even rhythm of his brother breathing across the cradle. He lay still and took stock of it the way he took stock of everything, reading the dark, confirming the quiet. Then he looked across at Andrew's cradle. Andrew was already looking back at him.

Neither made a sound. The understanding between them required none. It had no name and no plan and no objective beyond the fact that the world beyond the nursery existed and they had not yet been in it, which had always been sufficient reason to go.

Getting over the cradle rail took longer than it might have. Alex spent several minutes absorbed by the way moonlight moved across his own hand, which was genuinely interesting and worth the attention. Andrew became entirely focused on a loose thread at the edge of his blanket and pulled at it patiently and with total concentration, because the thread mattered, before eventually remembering where he was going. They reached the floor. The stone was cold. They noted it and moved on.

The hallway was dark and they crawled through it side by side, stopping at intervals not because they expected anything but because they understood in the wordless way they understood most things that they were not supposed to be there, and that this made being there considerably more worthwhile. The fortress breathed around them, ancient and indifferent. The soldiers on night watch moved in their usual patterns on the outer walls. Nothing in Thrace was paying any attention to two small boys making their way toward the courtyard. They found the war hounds near the training yard fires.

The hounds were not pets, and everyone in Thrace knew this without needing to be told. They were large and scarred and had been made for one purpose, and that purpose did not involve being approachable. They tolerated Ares because Ares was Ares. Venus they did not attack, and only because Ares was usually standing beside her. Everyone else in the fortress had long since developed the sensible habit of taking the long way around whenever the pack was in the yard.

The largest one raised its head when the twins appeared at the courtyard entrance. It had been reading threats for the length of its existence, and it read them now, steadily. Andrew smiled at it. Not strategically; there was nothing in him yet that operated with strategy. He simply smiled the way a small child smiled at something that interested him, openly and without any expectation of what came next.

The hound stood. Its tail began to move. Then the rest of the pack lifted their heads one by one and something passed through the yard that had no name, a settling, a recognition. The hounds had been waiting for something without knowing it, and here it was. They rose and came forward without wariness and arranged themselves around the twins quietly and deliberately, an honor guard finally given something worth guarding.

Alex grabbed a handful of fur. The animal it belonged to looked down at him with an expression that had no business on the face of a war hound. Andrew leaned against the largest one's flank and yawned. Alex found the nearest warm body and curled against it. The pack settled around them, and the fire burned lower, and in the fortress two empty cradles held their blankets in the dark, and their parents slept and knew nothing about it.

An hour before dawn Alex woke. He lay still for a moment taking stock, the way he took stock of things, reading the dark and the fire and the position of his brother. Andrew was asleep against the largest hound's flank, one hand still buried in its fur. The sky above the fortress wall had begun to change at the edge. He looked at Andrew. Andrew opened his eyes. They looked at each other across two feet of sleeping hound.

Alex started for the yard entrance. He made it as far as the gate before he looked back and found his brother exactly where he had left him, watching him with the calm blue eyes that belonged to neither of their parents, the hand still resting in the hound's fur, entirely settled. Alex considered the cradle. He considered his brother. Then he turned around and crawled back to the middle of the pack, and the largest hound shifted to make room without waking, and they slept through the rest of the dark and into the morning. They were still there when the sun came up and the first soldiers began their rounds.

It was a young guard on early patrol who found them. He stood at the entrance to the training yard long enough to make certain of what he was seeing. Then he went to find his lord and lady, moving quickly and carefully, because this outranked his authority to handle. "My lord. My lady," he said. "Your sons are in the training yard. They are with the hounds."

"With the hounds," Ares repeated. He was already on his feet and reaching for his sword, and it took him a moment to understand that a sword was not going to be any use. "Both of them?"

"Both of them, my lord." The guard kept his eyes carefully on the wall above Ares's shoulder. "Asleep, my lord. In the middle of the pack. The hounds will not let anyone near them."

Venus was already out of bed and reaching for her robe, her hair down, her feet bare, her face doing something he did not have time to read. She was at the door before he had decided what to do with the sword. He left it and followed her.

Ares came around the corner of the training yard and stopped. Venus stopped beside him. The pack filled the yard. Twenty war hounds arranged loosely and totally, with nowhere else to be, massive bodies draped across each other and the grass, and in the center of them slept Alex and Andrew. Andrew against the largest hound's side, one hand buried in black fur. Alex on his back with his face turned toward the morning sky, entirely at ease.

One of the younger soldiers stepped forward to get a better look. A growl moved through the pack, low and certain. The soldier stepped back. Ares moved forward.

The largest hound raised its head. Its tail came down against the grass once, a greeting. It recognized its master and was glad to see him, and then it lay still and did not move. Ares stopped two paces from the edge of the pack. He looked at the hound. The hound looked back at him with the full loyalty it had always shown, steady and without apology, and neither of them moved.

He tried again. Two deliberate steps, the kind that ended most things. Three more hounds raised their heads. No aggression, no teeth, nothing that could be answered, just a quiet wall of large, calm, entirely immovable animals that loved him completely and had decided that loving him and letting him through were not the same thing this morning. The hound at the center held his gaze. Its tail moved again. It was, he registered with some difficulty, genuinely glad to see him. It was simply not going to do anything about it.

He stood at the edge of his own training yard and looked at his sons sleeping in the middle of it. Behind him the soldiers had gone very quiet in the way they went quiet when they were paying close attention and had decided that appearing not to was the wiser approach. He could feel Venus watching from the entrance. He did not look at her. "Move," he said. The hound's tail thumped once against the grass. It did not move.

Venus laughed. It came out fully, without the management she usually applied when she was laughing at him specifically, and it carried across the yard and up into the clear Thracian morning and did not stop. She laughed until she had to put her hand against the wall. She tried once to collect herself, looked at him standing there in front of his own hounds with his arms at his sides and nothing to say, and that finished her. She pressed her hand over her mouth. It did not help. The soldiers on the outer wall found reasons to be elsewhere.

Ares did not move. He had held walls that should not have held. He had walked into things that made reasonable gods reconsider and had come out the other side and known what to do every time. He stood in the grass outside the circle his own animals had made and looked at his sons sleeping in the middle of it and had nothing. No angle. No approach. Not a word, because there was no word for this, for being kept out of your own yard by creatures that had been his since before these boys drew breath, who were looking at him now with the same eyes they had always used and simply were not going to let him past.

Venus wiped her eyes. She made a genuine effort. It lasted until she looked at him again.

In the center of the pack, Andrew woke. He pushed himself upright and looked across the yard at his father standing outside the circle, and at his mother at the entrance who was holding the wall for support, and he took in all of it with the calm blue eyes that had no business in the face of an infant and belonged to someone neither of his parents had named aloud. He looked for a long moment at the largest hound beside him. Something passed between them that no one watching could have named or explained. The hound held the boy's gaze. Then it stood and stepped quietly to one side, and around the yard the pack shifted, one by one, opening the circle the way a door opened when the person it had been waiting for finally arrived.

Ares walked in. He crossed the yard and crouched down beside his sons in the grass, and the hounds settled around him with their usual easy loyalty, tails moving, no hesitation, as though this had always been the arrangement and the morning had simply arrived at the part where it changed. Andrew looked up at him. Alex turned toward the sound of him without fully waking, the way he turned toward warmth. Ares stayed where he was. The hounds were warm around him and both his boys were within arm's reach and the morning was coming up gold over the walls of Thrace, and he did not examine any of it too closely, because examining it would require giving it a name and he was not ready to do that before the sun had finished rising.

Venus had gotten herself under control. Mostly. She stood in the entrance with her arms folded. She intended to remember this morning for the rest of her immortal life, and she was already deciding what to do with it.

Ares looked at her over his sons' heads. She pressed her lips together, and her shoulders moved. "Venus," he said, in the voice that had ended arguments on three continents.

"I am not saying anything," Venus said, saying everything. She kept her arms folded and her face very straight, and her eyes were still wet from laughing. She intended to say nothing about this morning for at least a century, and to enjoy every day of it.

Ares let it go. He looked down at Andrew, and then at the largest hound, which had not moved for its master and had moved for an infant. Something had passed between them in his own yard, and he did not yet know what it was.

Chapter TwoPink

Ares

He smelled it before he saw it. The war had run three weeks, which was unremarkable, and he had come home the way he always came home, through the main gate on horseback, armor still on. The gate guard had said nothing when he rode through. He understood now why.

The first thing he noticed was the courtyard. The stones were covered in something that caught the late afternoon light and threw it back in fragments, pink and gold and silver, as though someone had broken the sun into small pieces and distributed them across every surface of his fortress. It covered the steps. It covered the walls. A fine, dense, improbable layer of it across everything he could see, and where it had drifted into corners it had pooled to the consistency of damp sand, sunset-colored, if the sunset had made poor decisions.

Ares dismounted. He stood in the courtyard and turned slowly, reading it the way he read everything that did not immediately make sense, methodically and without committing to a conclusion before the evidence was complete. The glitter was not contained to the courtyard. It extended through the open doors into the corridor beyond. It covered the torches on the left wall in a way that suggested it had been applied with considerable enthusiasm from below. A trail of it led toward the east wing in a pattern that was not random: two sets of small handprints, moving together, which told him the direction and the method and did not yet tell him anything about the why.

Then he heard it. A sound from the direction of the laundry, which was in the east wing, which was where the handprints were going. Not distress. Not words. A rhythmic thudding, like something striking wicker from the inside, accompanied by a voice he recognized employing a register he had not heard from it before. She had been managing a situation for some time, by the sound of it, and had arrived at the part where managing it was no longer the primary concern. He followed the handprints.

The laundry room had received the full treatment. Every surface. The ceiling, which implied either a great deal of force or a great deal of creativity, and he had sons who were one month old and walking, so he was prepared to accept either. In the center of the room sat a large wicker laundry basket. It was shaking slightly. From inside it came the voice of the Goddess of Love and Beauty, which had by this point dispensed with the register he had found in the corridor and arrived at something considerably more direct. "Ares."

He stopped in the doorway. He looked at the basket. He looked at the room, the trail of pink handprints leading from the basket to the window and up the outer wall to the roof. He looked at the basket again. He kept his voice at a register he had used on battlefields for two thousand years, the register that gave nothing away. "What are you doing?"

A pause from inside the basket. It was the pause of a goddess choosing, from a great many available responses, the one that would do the least damage to her marriage. "I am in a laundry basket, Ares."

"I can see that." Something was happening in his chest that he was managing with considerable effort. "I thought laundry belonged in the basket. I think you may have this backward. Are you hurt?"

"I am not hurt," she said. The basket gave one small, eloquent shake. "I am, however, still in it."

"Are you," he began.

"The latch," she said, very precisely, having had considerable time to think about how to say this, "is on the outside."

"I can see that as well," Ares said. He could.

Another pause. "Ares."

"The boys," he started. Through the doorway the three perfectly pink war hounds had arranged themselves in a row and were watching him calmly. They had no opinion about their new color.

"On the roof," she said. "They have been on the roof for some time. Ares."

"Yes."

"Open the basket."

"Yes," he said. "I am coming to you now." He pushed off the doorframe and crossed the room toward her. He made it four steps.

His boot found the puddle of eternal luminescence that had migrated from the original site of the incident to the floor between the doorway and the basket, and it found it in full stride. He had been given no reason to expect the floor to be anything other than a floor. His feet went. All of him went. He was airborne for a moment, and his body, which had not been airborne unintentionally in two thousand years, took offense. Then he hit the laundry room floor with the full weight of the God of War. A cloud of glitter rose around him in a pink and gold bloom, then settled slowly back down across his armor, his face, and the entirety of his considerable person.

He lay on his back and looked at the ceiling. The ceiling was covered in glitter. He was, he was aware, also covered in glitter. These two facts existed together in his mind clearly. Both were simply true and could not be made otherwise.

From inside the basket came silence. Not a sleeping silence. She had heard a very large god hit a stone floor at considerable speed, and she was exercising a restraint he could not have managed in her position. He could hear her breathing, very evenly, on purpose.

"Ares," she said, after a moment.

He looked at the ceiling. "Yes."

"Are you on the floor?" She asked it with the careful neutrality of someone who already knew the answer.

"Yes." There did not seem to be anything else to say about it.

Another silence from the basket, longer this time. "The goo," she said. It was not a question.

"Yes," said the God of War, from the floor. He did not elaborate. There was glitter in his mouth.

He lay there. His armor, which had survived two thousand years of warfare, was covered in glitter. The thing that had been building in him since the courtyard arrived all at once and there was nothing to do with it but let it out, and he laughed. Flat on his back in his own laundry room in full armor, he laughed until the ceiling blurred and the glitter on his face was no longer entirely from the floor. From the roof his sons added their voices to it, cheerful and entirely unrepentant. They had been waiting for exactly this moment, and it had exceeded everything they had hoped for.

He lay there and let it run its course and somewhere in the middle of it a thought arrived that he did not examine too closely because examining it would have required stopping and he was not ready to stop. A year ago this fortress had been what it had always been: stone and silence and the cold of a place built for one purpose and used for nothing else. He had walked these corridors for centuries and the sound of his own footsteps had been the primary sound. No one had laughed in these rooms. There had been no reason to.

Venus was in the basket. The hounds were pink. His sons were on the roof. The fortress smelled of divine cosmetics and of a warmth that had not been here before, which he was not prepared to name. All of it had arrived in the space of a year. None of it had been planned. All of it was, he understood lying on the floor in his armor, exactly what this place had always needed, and he had not known he was waiting for it until it was already here.

From inside the basket, Venus said nothing. She said nothing for the entire duration. He could feel her in there, sitting in the dark with her arms folded and her dignity assembled around her, listening to her husband laugh himself to tears on the laundry room floor, and making the deliberate choice not to participate. That restraint, he thought, was extraordinary. He was not capable of it now and it was happening to him.

He lay there until he could breathe again. Then he got up, which took longer than it should have because the floor was still the floor, and crossed the remaining distance to the basket without incident, and opened the latch. Venus emerged with her dignity intact, having decided that whatever had just happened was going to be handled on her terms and no one else's. She was covered in glitter. Her hair had achieved a state he did not have a word for. There was pink goo on her left shoulder. She stood upright, smoothed her dress with both hands, and looked at him.

He looked back at her. She looked at him for a long moment. She looked at the floor. She looked back at him. "The goo," she said, "was directly in front of the basket."

"I was not looking at the floor," Ares said. He had been looking at the basket. He had been looking at her.

"No," she said. "You were not." She let that sit in the glittering air between them for exactly as long as it deserved.

She smoothed her dress again. Her expression was doing the work of several things at once and doing it with considerable discipline. He recognized her restraint and understood that she was not going to give him the satisfaction of laughing, which was its own kind of dignity and which he respected entirely, and which was also, he thought, going to last approximately until she was alone.

He wiped his eyes again and looked around the room, the glitter, the pink goo, the handprints going up the wall to the window, and the laughter settled into something warmer and quieter. Then he looked at the basket. He looked at it for a moment longer than he had looked at anything else in the room.

The latch was on the outside. That was not unusual for a laundry basket. What was unusual was the angle of it, the way it had seated into its catch. It had not fallen shut. It had been closed. He had spent two thousand years reading the difference between accident and intent on battlefields and in rooms and in faces that were trying not to show him things, and what he was looking at was not an accident.

The laughter was gone. He stood in the glittered wreckage of the laundry room and looked at the basket. His sons were one month old. They were walking, they had eyes that belonged to someone neither of their parents had named aloud, and they had understood cause and effect well enough to close a latch. To close it with intent. To close it and leave. He did not say anything for a moment.

Venus was watching him. He could feel her silence change when his did. She had been waiting for him to arrive at the same place she had been sitting since the basket closed. She had carried it alone in the dark, and now she was watching him pick it up for the first time. "Venus," he said.

"I know," she said. He looked at her. She looked back at him steadily. She had felt the latch click from the inside and sat in the dark with what it meant. She had not found a name for it either, and she was not going to pretend otherwise.

Outside, from the direction of the roof, came unmistakable laughter. It was small and delighted and entirely unrepentant, two voices carrying down through the walls of the fortress. The owners had been watching for some time and were very pleased with how things had gone. Ares looked at the window. He looked at Venus. She had been in a laundry basket for the better part of an hour and was not yet ready to find any of this as funny as he had found it. She was perhaps getting there. "They are on the roof," he said.

"They have been on the roof," Venus said, "for some time." She said it the way she had said it from inside the basket, with an hour of practice behind it. Above them, a small voice laughed again, and then another joined it.

Venus

It had started with the makeup bag. This was the thing she kept returning to, in the basket and afterward, the moment when she had looked away. Not for long. She had set the bag on the vanity table to find Andrew, who had been in the corridor a moment ago and was now not, because Andrew had developed in his fourth week of life the ability to be somewhere and then not be there, which was a quality Venus was not certain she had fully accounted for when she imagined what having children would be like.

She found Andrew in the corridor examining a loose floor stone with the focused attention he brought to everything that interested him. She brought him back, set him down, turned around. Alex had the makeup bag.

She did not panic. "Alex," she said, "that is mine." She crossed the room at a measured speed. She had been managing these two for a month, and she had learned that the interval between acquiring something interesting and doing something irreversible with it was shorter than it should have been but not always zero. She was almost there when Alex opened the bag and Andrew, who had followed her back across the room without her noticing, sat down on her foot.

The pink goo was called something in the language of divine cosmetics that roughly translated to eternal luminescence, which was accurate in the sense that it did not come off easily from anything it touched. Alex discovered this quality approximately two seconds after opening the jar, at which point the quality became academic. He had the expression he wore when something exceeded his expectations, which was an expression Venus had learned to read as a warning and had not read fast enough this time.

Andrew watched his brother with the calm, considering attention he brought to Alex's experiments before he decided whether to participate. He decided to participate. She had dealt with worse. She had the situation largely in hand within several minutes, or something that resembled it, which was the available option.

The war hounds were the complication she had not anticipated. The three youngest came in from the courtyard, following the sound of the boys. They were fond of the boys in the total and entirely uncritical way the whole pack was fond of them, and of no one else in the room. They went past Venus with a long, flat look that she returned with great care, and she kept very still until they had settled around her sons. They did not recognize a crisis. Alex saw them and his face did the thing it did when he had a new idea.

She was not entirely certain how the pink goo got onto the first hound. She had a theory involving trajectory and enthusiasm that she had not yet shared with anyone, because sharing it would require describing the sequence of events in a way that did not reflect well on her management of the situation. The first hound received it and looked at itself with mild interest. The other two came over to investigate. Alex and Andrew were delighted. The hounds, who were not creatures given to vanity, appeared completely unbothered by the color.

Venus had one eye on the boys and one eye on three increasingly pink war hounds and was reaching for something to manage the situation with when her foot found the puddle of eternal luminescence that had migrated from the original site of the incident to the floor directly behind her. It was, as advertised, extremely slippery. Her feet went in different directions with a speed that did not allow for any corrective action. She was airborne for a moment that felt longer than it was, and then the laundry basket, which had been sitting open behind her, received her. It had been in exactly the right place at exactly the wrong time.

The basket tipped. The lid came down. She heard the latch.

She lay in the dark for a moment taking stock. She was not injured. The basket was solid. The latch, she understood from the sound it had made and the way it had seated, was closed, and from the inside of the basket there was no way to reach the outside of it, which meant she was going to be in the basket until someone opened it.

She heard the boys. She heard the kind of quiet that meant they were looking at something and deciding what to do about it, and then she heard the sound of small hands on wicker, methodical and purposeful, moving not toward the latch but away from it, toward the window, toward the courtyard, toward wherever they were going next. She lay in the basket and knew what she knew and did not have the language for it yet. The latch had not fallen and had not been bumped. She turned it over in the dark the way she turned difficult things over, not looking for the answer but learning the shape of the question, and outside the basket Thrace continued its afternoon. The hounds moved through the fortress a beautiful shade of pink. Her sons went wherever they were going and left her to it.

She had been in the basket for some time before she heard Ares come through the gate. She heard him laugh, full and unmanaged. He had not expected to come home to this, and he found it extraordinary. Something in her that had been wound tight since the latch clicked began, slowly, to release. She had not said anything about the latch. Not in the basket and not after. She knew what she knew. He knew what he knew. They would find the language for it when they had it. Until then there was the latch, and two boys a month old on the roof.
