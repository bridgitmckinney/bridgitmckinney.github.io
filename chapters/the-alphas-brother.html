Chapter OneThree Is a Number

Ryder Kane knew exactly what happened when he looked directly at a woman from the stage. The first row always reacted before the rest of the arena because proximity made people honest, and bright blue eyes under concert lights apparently counted as an unfair advantage. Tonight, a blonde in a silver top stopped singing halfway through the chorus when his gaze found hers, while the brunette beside her grabbed her arm and screamed. Ryder smiled because the reaction was familiar, harmless, and useful when twelve thousand people had paid good money for a country show and the chance to believe every song might somehow be about them.

The final chorus hit with the full band behind him, fiddle and steel guitar riding over the drums while thousands of voices shouted the lyrics back at him. Ryder stood beneath the white sweep of the spotlights with his acoustic guitar slung across his chest, dark hair damp at his temples and his black shirt sticking between his shoulder blades. The stage was hot, the room was deafening, and he loved every damn second of it. Nothing matched standing in that much noise and knowing he had created it.

He let the last chord ring until it disappeared beneath the roar of the audience, then stepped toward the microphone while sweat slipped down the side of his neck. Phones glowed across the arena, signs waved above heads, and several women along the barricade were doing their best to make certain he noticed them before he left. “You’ve been trouble all night,” Ryder told the crowd, which naturally made them louder. He laughed, pushed his hair back with one hand, and added, “I approve.”

Behind him, his drummer made a sharp cutting motion across his throat to remind Ryder they had already gone past the scheduled ending. Ryder saw him, deliberately looked away, and picked out the opening lick of another song. The arena erupted before he finished the first measure, and his drummer’s expression promised violence later. Ryder grinned at him and kept playing because curfews existed primarily to give tour managers high blood pressure.

Twenty minutes later, he finally walked offstage into a wall of movement. A stagehand took his guitar, another person put a bottle of water in his hand, and Marcus, his tour manager, started discussing tomorrow’s schedule before Ryder had finished drinking. His pulse was still running with the concert, his ears were ringing, and the wolf beneath his skin was riding the same satisfied current. Ryder listened long enough to establish that nobody needed him for the next several hours, which was the only part of the conversation he cared about.

“Perfect. I’m going to behave,” Ryder said, wiping his face with a towel while Marcus narrowed his eyes at him. Marcus had known him too long to believe a word of that sentence and informed him that he had never once announced his intention to behave immediately before actually doing it. Ryder considered the accusation before giving him an innocent shrug. “Statistically, that means I’m due.”

Marcus stared at him a moment longer before pointing toward his dressing room. “Shower, change, and remember there will be photographers at the club. Try not to look like you just crawled out of a bar fight.” Ryder told him he had looked excellent after several bar fights, but Marcus was already walking away. He watched him go and decided management had made him unnecessarily cynical.

His black leather jacket waited across the back of a chair inside the dressing room. The BLACK HEART PACK emblem covered the back, a red heart caught inside silver barbed wire with the pack name curved around it. It was not stage wardrobe, even if photographers treated it like one, and it certainly was not merchandise despite repeated requests from the label to reproduce it. The jacket meant something that could not be purchased beside a concert T-shirt.

Black Heart was home, and Ryder had never stopped belonging to it simply because he spent most of his year somewhere else. He loved the mountains, his brother, his pack, and the knowledge that somewhere deep in the Appalachians there was always a place that belonged to him. He also loved music, motorcycles, hotel suites, unfamiliar cities, and waking up without anyone deciding where he needed to be next. Ryder saw no contradiction between those things and had no interest in inventing one.

An hour later, his motorcycle rolled beneath the awning of one of the most exclusive clubs in the city. The public line wrapped around the side of the building, but Ryder never went near it because security opened a private entrance the moment he pulled up. Camera flashes erupted from the sidewalk when somebody recognized him before the heavy door closed behind him. Ryder handed his helmet to security, adjusted his jacket, and followed the pulse of music upstairs.

The entire private level had been reserved for the after-party. Dark glass overlooked the dance floor below, bottles waited in silver buckets beside curved velvet booths, and everyone in the room looked as though somebody had personally inspected them before approving their invitation. The women were beautiful, the liquor was expensive, and the music was low enough for conversation without ruining the atmosphere. Ryder decided whoever had chosen the place deserved a raise.

He had never seen the point in pretending beautiful women did not interest him. They did, often and enthusiastically, and the interest had always been mutual enough that nobody needed to invent tragedy around it. Some men behaved as though enjoying sex required a secret wound, a broken heart, or unresolved childhood damage to make the behavior respectable. Ryder simply liked women, liked being wanted, and had yet to discover a convincing reason to apologize for either one.

A redhead in a black dress caught him near the bar before he finished his first drink. She was tall, gorgeous, and direct enough to slide one hand across the front of his jacket while asking whether the stories about musicians were true. Ryder looked down at her hand before meeting her eyes again. “Depends on which stories you heard.”

“The interesting ones,” she said, leaning close enough that he caught the warm edge of her perfume. Ryder told her those were usually the reliable ones, and she laughed like he had given the correct answer. She introduced herself as Tessa and explained that she had been at the show from the second row. Ryder remembered seeing her, which pleased her considerably more than she tried to hide.

They had been talking for less than ten minutes when another woman approached. Sloane was a brunette in a dark blue dress, and Ryder recognized her as one of the women from the front row before she introduced herself. She looked genuinely surprised when he mentioned where she had been standing. “You actually remember people from the stage?”

“I remember interesting things,” Ryder said, watching her smile spread. Tessa glanced between them, plainly deciding whether she had just acquired competition. Ryder noticed the assessment and took another drink without interfering. If they wanted to compete, he was perfectly willing to enjoy the show.

The third woman arrived before either of them could establish anything resembling territory. Nadia had dark curls, warm brown skin, and a green dress that made half the room look underdressed by comparison. She greeted Ryder like they had already settled something privately, kissed his cheek, and accepted a drink from the bartender without asking permission from anyone. Tessa watched her with narrowed eyes while Sloane shifted half a step closer to Ryder.

The competition became significantly less subtle once they moved toward one of the private booths. Tessa claimed the seat beside Ryder before anyone else could reach it, and Sloane immediately sat on his other side. Nadia looked at the arrangement, smiled to herself, and settled across from him with one leg crossed slowly over the other. Ryder settled into the leather seat and wondered how long they intended to pretend any of this was casual.

“So what happens after this?” Tessa asked, letting one hand settle comfortably against Ryder’s thigh. Sloane looked down at the hand and commented that subtlety appeared to have died sometime during the concert, which earned her a sharp smile from Tessa. “I wasn’t trying to be subtle,” Tessa replied. Across from them, Nadia sipped her drink and asked whether either woman realized Ryder could actually hear the conversation.

Ryder covered his smile with the edge of his glass because openly enjoying himself would probably make matters worse. His wolf was awake beneath his skin, amused by the attention but no more attached to any one woman than Ryder was. There was no strange pull, no recognition, and certainly no instinct announcing that one of these women had been created specifically for him. Ryder had never found his mate, and the absence had never felt like a missing piece of himself.

Tessa eventually turned toward him and told him to settle the matter. Ryder asked what he was supposed to settle while Sloane leaned forward with obvious interest. “Which one of us is leaving with you?” Tessa asked, apparently deciding indirect conversation had become tiresome. Nadia lowered her glass and watched Ryder.

Ryder looked at Tessa first, then Sloane, then Nadia. Tessa was openly daring him to choose her, Sloane looked certain she could change any answer she disliked, and Nadia appeared entertained by all of it. Every option in front of him was attractive. Ryder took his time with his drink while the women waited for the verdict they seemed to think he was required to deliver.

“I’m not sure why I have to pick one,” he said, and silence settled over the booth for approximately two seconds. Nadia smiled first, understanding where his thoughts had gone, while Sloane’s eyebrows climbed almost to her hairline. Tessa stared at Ryder as though determining whether she had heard him correctly. Ryder sat back and let the idea reach all three of them.

Tessa finally pointed between herself and the other two women. “You mean all three of us?” There was no offense in her voice, only disbelief mixed with curiosity. Ryder nodded once because he saw no reason to pretend he meant anything else. “At the same time?” she clarified.

“That was the idea,” Ryder said, and Nadia laughed softly from across the table. Sloane looked from Ryder to the other women, reassessing the night under entirely different terms. Ryder let them think without pushing because a question only remained an invitation if everyone was free to refuse it. Tessa’s hand stayed on his thigh, which he considered an encouraging sign.

Nadia tilted her head. “You’re remarkably calm about proposing that.” Ryder told her he had not proposed anything remotely permanent, which made Sloane laugh. Tessa asked whether this sort of arrangement was normal for him, and Ryder shrugged rather than manufacturing a reputation even larger than the one he already had. “Normal is a boring word.”

The three women looked at one another again, and Ryder realized the argument over who got him had transformed into an entirely different kind of negotiation. Tessa looked Sloane over without apology, then did the same to Nadia. Sloane returned the inspection without embarrassment, and Nadia appeared amused enough to enjoy making both of them squirm. Whatever decision they reached needed to belong to them as much as it belonged to Ryder.

Before anybody could answer, Ryder set his glass down and leaned forward. “One thing first. If anybody comes upstairs with me tonight, understand exactly what this is.” The teasing faded enough that all three women gave him their attention. Ryder had learned years ago that being clear before clothes started disappearing saved everyone unnecessary trouble later.

“This is one night,” Ryder continued. “I’m not looking for a girlfriend, I’m not starting a relationship, and I’m not going to wake up tomorrow suddenly talking about where this is headed. There is no headed.” Tessa’s expression remained interested while Sloane listened without interruption. Nadia gave a small approving nod.

Ryder rested his forearms against his knees and made sure none of them mistook charm for a loophole. “You’ll be treated well, nobody does anything they don’t want to do, and if someone changes her mind at any point, it stops. But I’m not promising another night, and I’m not going to lie tonight just because it might make tomorrow easier.” He had never understood men who said whatever was necessary to get a woman into bed and then acted shocked when she expected the promise to mean something afterward.

Tessa considered him. “So this is your speech.” Ryder told her it was less a speech and more an attempt to avoid anyone throwing a drink at him over breakfast. That earned a laugh from Sloane, while Nadia said she appreciated a man who labeled the package before delivery. Ryder nearly spit out the last of his whiskey.

Sloane tapped one manicured nail against her glass. “And if one of us decides tomorrow she wants more?” Ryder answered without hesitation because that was the question he wanted asked now rather than twelve hours later. “Then I tell her the same thing I’m telling you tonight.”

“You really don’t soften anything, do you?” Sloane asked, though she looked more intrigued than insulted. Ryder told her he preferred disappointing someone with the truth before anything happened over disappointing her with a lie afterward. Nadia raised her glass toward him in approval. Tessa finally smiled and said at least nobody could accuse him of false advertising.

“Exactly,” Ryder said, relaxing again once the point had been made. “Now everybody knows what’s on the table.” Nadia corrected him that everybody currently knew what might be happening on several pieces of furniture, which made Tessa cough into her drink. Ryder stared at Nadia for one stunned second before laughing hard enough to earn looks from the neighboring booth.

The laughter broke what remained of the tension. Sloane laughed until she had to wipe beneath one eye, while Tessa accused Nadia of being worse than Ryder. Nadia accepted the accusation like a compliment and announced that she had already made her decision. When Sloane asked what that decision was, Nadia stood from the booth and held one hand toward Ryder.

“I’m in,” Nadia said, and there was nothing hesitant about her expression. Ryder accepted her hand and stood, giving the other women space to make their own decisions without assuming anything from the earlier flirting. Tessa looked from Ryder to Nadia, finished her drink, and stood as well. “I did not fight this hard for his attention just to let her have the interesting part.”

Sloane remained seated for two seconds longer, making a show of considering them. Ryder did not reach for her or coax her because he had already made his invitation clear. She finally smiled, placed her glass on the table, and rose. “I’m only coming because somebody has to supervise the three of you.”

“Of course,” Ryder said solemnly. “Public service.” Several people noticed when the four of them left the booth together. Ryder’s fiddle player was standing near the bar and nearly choked on his drink when he counted the women walking with him. Ryder caught his eye and lifted three fingers without slowing down. His fiddle player mouthed something that was probably insulting, while Ryder answered with a grin.

The private elevator waited beyond a guarded hallway at the rear of the club. Ryder stepped inside with Nadia, Tessa, and Sloane following him, and the mirrored walls reflected four adults who had made very deliberate decisions about how the rest of the night would go. The club maintained private suites on the floors above for guests who valued discretion, a feature Ryder had appreciated the moment Marcus mentioned it. He pressed the button and watched the doors slide closed.

Tessa leaned against the mirrored wall and studied him with a smile that suggested the competition had not disappeared simply because the terms had changed. “So if we’re all clear that nobody is auditioning for Mrs. Ryder Kane, I have another question.” Sloane groaned that she already knew she was not going to like this, while Nadia folded her arms and waited. Ryder told Tessa to ask. “Which one of us gets first choice?”

Ryder turned his head slowly toward her. Sloane immediately objected that Tessa had somehow turned a shared evening back into a competition, and Nadia pointed out that none of them had actually established what first choice meant. The three women began arguing again while the elevator carried them toward the private floor. Ryder rested his shoulders against the glass, listened for a moment, and realized that choosing all three had solved absolutely nothing.

The elevator chimed as it reached their floor. The doors began to open, but none of the women had stopped arguing long enough to notice. Ryder looked out at the empty corridor, then back at the three beautiful women who had apparently decided he was going to need rules for an evening he had intended to spend breaking them. Whatever happened once they reached the suite, this discussion was not finished.

Chapter TwoGin and Sin

The private elevator inside the luxury pavilion did not rush, and neither did Ryder Kane. It climbed with a low hydraulic hum, lifting him away from the bass of the lounge below and into a world where he set the terms. Some men required a frantic chase to validate themselves, a desperate series of promises whispered in dark corners to coax a woman out of a crowded room. Ryder found that pathetic. True control did not beg, and it certainly did not perform for attention it had already won. He stood dead center in the small car, his broad shoulders relaxed under his thin black shirt, a quiet smile playing at the edge of his mouth as he watched the three women who had chosen to step inside with him. The territorial posturing and sharp glances that had defined their initial interaction down at the marble bar had entirely evaporated the moment the steel doors closed them off from the rest of the world. It had been replaced by mutual understanding, with no false pretenses and no hidden agendas. They were three exceptionally beautiful, intelligent, grown women who knew exactly whose elevator they were in, and they were completely on board with the rules of the game he had laid out. They liked his honesty and the absence of strings hanging over the night.

When Ryder swiped the key card at the end of the corridor and pushed open the double doors of the club’s private top-floor suite, the sprawling grid of amber headlights and white neon below felt like a distant stage prop behind the thick, soundproofed glass paneling. The suite itself was an expansive, open-concept masterpiece of dark hardwood floors, polished obsidian surfaces, and minimalist design that emphasized the dizzying height of his vantage point over the city. Ryder walked across the polished foyer and tossed the key card onto the dark marble surface of the kitchen island, the small click of it cutting through the silence of the room. He reached back and shed his heavy black leather jacket, draping the flawless, unmarred hide over the back of an upholstered barstool. The blood-red Black Heart crest caught the low amber light, a stark crimson against the clean, modern lines of the room. The patch meant home. It meant his brother, his pack, and a place deep in the mountains that would always belong to him, and Ryder had never once treated it like a costume. Tonight, he simply set it aside to make room for pure, unadulterated luxury. “Make yourselves at home, ladies,” Ryder murmured, his gravelly baritone smooth and unhurried as he walked behind the long, curved marble bar that dominated the western wing of the suite.

Nadia didn’t hesitate for a single second. She was a creature of unwavering confidence, and that was exactly why Ryder had noticed her in the first place. She reached back with a fluid movement, unbuckled the delicate straps of her emerald-green heels, and stepped out of them with a soft, satisfied sigh of pure relief. She slid onto one of the tall leather-and-chrome barstools, propping her chin in her hands while her dark curls cascaded heavily over her bare shoulders, her eyes tracking his every movement with open curiosity. Across the expansive hardwood floor, Tessa and Sloane walked toward the glass, their dark silhouettes framed dramatically against the glowing metropolis outside. They stood close enough that their shoulders brushed, sharing a quiet, private laugh that had absolutely nothing to do with jealousy and everything to do with the shared, reckless thrill of a boundary-pushing night. They were validating each other’s choices without uttering a word, fully stepping into the carefree environment Ryder created wherever he laid his head.

Ryder watched them through the reflection of the glass cabinets behind the bar, his inner wolf thoroughly pleased by the total absence of friction in his personal space. He liked order, but he loved an untamed, clean simplicity even more. He opened the under-counter stainless steel refrigerator and pulled out a heavy, heavily frosted bottle of premium dry gin, a handful of fresh, dark-skinned limes from a crystal bowl, and a tall glass pitcher filled to the brim with sweet, tart juice. He didn’t use measuring jiggers or small shot glasses. A man who measured his liquor was a man who didn’t trust his own instincts, and Ryder’s instincts had never once failed him, whether he was picking out a melody on an old acoustic, navigating a tight curve on a dark highway, or reading the desires of a woman standing across from him.

He gripped the heavy metal cocktail shaker, dropping large, solid cubes of ice into the bottom until the cold steel rang out like a bell in the quiet kitchen. With a practiced, heavy hand, he poured the gin, the clear, aromatic liquor splashing generously over the ice before he drowned it in the thick, vibrant juice from the pitcher. He picked up a slender silver bar knife, his movements fluid and efficient as he sliced the limes into thick, bleeding wheels, dropping them straight into the mix without looking down. He slammed the metal tin together with the heel of his palm and began to shake. The sharp, rapid rattle of the ice inside the steel filled the quiet suite, a rhythmic, clean sound that drew Tessa and Sloane away from the window and straight toward the bar like a magnet. They moved with an easy grace, their dresses shifting against their skin, eyes locked onto the casual confidence radiating from the man behind the counter.

“Gin and juice?” Tessa teased, stepping away from the glass and joining Nadia at the marble counter. She leaned her forearms against the cool stone, her red hair spilling over her shoulders as her fingers slid slowly across the surface, intentionally lingering close to where Ryder was working. “I have to admit, Kane, I figured a platinum-selling country star with a sold-out arena tour would be popping thousand-dollar bottles of vintage champagne for an after-party in a place like this.”

Ryder stopped shaking, the metal tins coated in a thick, velvety layer of white frost. He separated the shakers with a crisp, echoing pop, pouring the pale, ice-cold liquid into three tall, frosted highball glasses he had lined up on the counter. He pushed the drinks forward, one by one, ensuring his gaze locked directly with each woman as the cold glass reached her hand. He leaned his forearms against his side of the marble, his sapphire blue eyes flashing with a wicked, predatory spark beneath the dim pendant lights.

“Trust me, sweetheart,” Ryder said, his voice dropping into a rough, intimate register that made Sloane go still for a beat as she took her glass. “Champagne is for long, boring conversations you want to forget by morning. Gin will make you sin.”

Nadia threw her head back, a loud, rich laugh escaping her lips as she grabbed her glass and took a long, deep drink without waiting for a toast. “God, I love an honest man. No games, no tragic backstories, just a good drink and a promise he intends to keep.”

“No games at all,” Sloane echoed softly, sliding her barstool a fraction of an inch closer until her bare arm brushed against the soft cotton of Ryder’s black shirt. The warmth of her skin was an immediate, grounding contrast to the freezing condensation coating the outside of the glasses. Ryder did not move away.

Ryder topped off Nadia’s glass before it was half empty. “Tell me something,” he said, looking at Tessa. “You were in the second row all night. What did I miss?”

“Your drummer,” Tessa said immediately. “When you started that extra song, he looked like he was going to climb over the kit and strangle you with a cymbal stand.” Nadia set her glass down, pulled her mouth into a flat, furious line, and widened her eyes until a vein nearly stood out in her forehead. She jabbed two invisible drumsticks at an invisible wristwatch. It was so exactly his drummer that Ryder laughed out loud, and Sloane had to put her forehead down on the cool marble to recover. “That’s him,” Ryder said when he could talk again. “That’s the face. He makes it at least twice a week.”

Sloane lifted her head from the marble and asked whether the drummer ever won. “Never once,” Ryder said. Ryder poured the second round with the same heavy hand as the first, and none of them objected. “So what do the three of you do when you aren’t heckling musicians from the front rows?”

Tessa sold commercial real estate and described a rooftop lease with the same hungry enthusiasm most people saved for dessert. Nadia taught contemporary dance at a studio downtown, and it showed even when she was sitting still, every gesture finished all the way to the tips of her fingers. Sloane took a slow sip and said she was an ER nurse who had traded two shifts to be at the concert tonight. Ryder asked whether that meant she was secretly assessing everyone in the room, and she told him she had clocked his sprained left wrist from the second song. Ryder stopped with the bottle in his hand. “Nobody noticed that.”

“I noticed,” Sloane said. “You favor it on the downstroke. It’s subtle, but it’s there.”

Ryder flexed the wrist under the pendant lights to prove the point. “It healed because I’m a wolf. Give it another day.”

Sloane’s gaze drifted past him to the leather jacket hanging over the barstool, the blood-red heart facing the room. Nobody in the suite looked surprised by the word. Werewolves had been on the evening news for longer than any of them had been alive, and a woman who worked emergency medicine had almost certainly stitched up a few. She studied him with open professional interest. “Is it true you run hot?”

“Warmer than average,” Ryder said, and Tessa looked ready to verify it personally. She reached across the marble and laid her palm flat against his forearm, then left it there considerably longer than any medical assessment required. “That is not warmer than average,” she said. “That is a space heater.”

Nadia nodded toward the jacket. “That isn’t a label thing, is it? The patch.”

“No.” Ryder glanced at the heart and the silver wire. “That’s my pack. Black Heart. Deep in the Appalachians, a lot of trees, a lot of wolves, my brother, and no cell reception in half the places worth going.” His voice softened on the word brother before he could stop it, and Nadia noticed. She did not push. She only smiled at him over her glass like she had filed it somewhere, and Ryder liked her a great deal for letting it go. Tessa was less restrained. “Are you somebody important up there?” she asked, in the same teasing tone people used to ask celebrities whether they did their own grocery shopping.

Ryder saw no reason to be coy about that part. “My brother’s the Alpha.” Sloane tilted her head, studying him. “And you?”

“I’m the one who sings,” Ryder said. It was true, and it was also the shortest honest answer available. The longer one was that he was next in line to lead the largest pack in the country, and every wolf who mattered in Black Heart knew it, whether he was home or halfway across the country with a guitar on his back. He saw no reason to bring that into a hotel suite at one in the morning.

By the third round, the distance between them had stopped making sense. Tessa’s hand had never really left his arm, and Sloane had drifted close enough that her shoulder rested easily against his. Nadia slid off her barstool, set her empty glass on the marble, and crossed the few feet between them with the unhurried confidence of a woman who had already made up her mind. She stopped in front of Ryder, glanced at the two women on either side of him, and raised one eyebrow. “The gin is working,” she said.

“I did warn you.” Ryder looked down at her and did not reach for her. He had meant every word he said downstairs, and none of it had changed on the ride up, so he waited.

Nadia rose onto her toes and kissed him. It was warm and certain and tasted faintly of juice and lime, and when she finally pulled back, Tessa made a small sound of approval that was only partly a joke. Sloane set her drink down with great care, as if she were clearing the way for something, and turned his face toward hers with two fingers beneath his jaw. She kissed like she was finishing an argument she fully intended to win. Tessa, when her turn came, laughed against his mouth first and then stopped laughing altogether.

It was Tessa who finally stepped back and held out one hand toward the hallway. Sloane took it. Nadia took Ryder’s. The four of them left a trail across the suite that housekeeping would discover in the morning, a pair of emerald heels beside the bar, a black dress over the back of a chair, and a black shirt abandoned halfway down the hall. At the bedroom door, Ryder paused long enough to look at each of them in turn. Nobody hesitated, and Nadia pulled him through.

The master bedroom was a vast, low-lit space dominated by a king-sized bed positioned directly opposite another sprawling wall of soundproofed glass, giving the absolute illusion that they were floating directly over the glowing heart of the sleeping city. The hours that followed were loud, unhurried, and considerably more fun than anyone had planned. There was laughter in them, which Ryder had always considered the mark of a good night rather than a distraction from one, and one breathless stretch when all three of them conspired against him so thoroughly that he gave up any pretense of being in charge. It was wild, it was exhausting, and it left absolutely no room for drama, second-guessing, or regret.

By four o’clock in the morning, the heavy heat of the bedroom had finally settled into a quiet, rhythmic calm. The thick velvet curtains were drawn halfway across the glass, letting a soft, amber-gray pre-dawn light bleed lazily across the tangled mess of white sheets and discarded clothing on the floor. Nadia and Sloane were fast asleep on either side of him, their bodies relaxed and soft in deep repose, dead to the world after the long night. Tessa was curled up directly across his bare chest, her red hair splayed like spilled wine against his shoulder, her breathing a slow, steady, comforting rise and fall against his ribs.

Ryder lay perfectly still in the center of the bed, his head propped comfortably against a stack of plush pillows as he stared quietly up at the dark shadows dancing across the ceiling. His pulse had settled back into a lazy, powerful metronome, the steady thud of his heart echoing slightly in the quiet room. He felt satisfied, unburdened, and entirely whole. He had a custom-built motorcycle downstairs in the private garage that could outrun anything on the highway, a bank account that ensured he never had to answer to a single soul alive, and a life that belonged entirely, beautifully to himself. There were no chains on him, no expectations waiting for him when the sun finally cleared the horizon, and no one in the world who had the right to tell him where he needed to be next. He was a free man, living a perfect life, entirely on his own terms.

His phone buzzed. The sound came from somewhere on the floor, a flat, insistent vibration against the hardwood, muffled by whatever pile of clothing had swallowed his jeans. Ryder ignored it. The label called at strange hours, his manager called at worse ones, and nothing in the world could not wait until a civilized time. The buzzing stopped. Three seconds later, it started again.

Tessa murmured a sleepy complaint against his chest. Ryder eased out from under her, slid her onto the warm pillow he left behind, and found his jeans by the foot of the bed. The phone was still vibrating in the back pocket when he pulled it free, and the screen lit his hand in the dark with a single word. RHETT.

Ryder frowned at it. His brother went to bed at a responsible hour, woke at a responsible hour, and had never once in his life called at four in the morning to talk. Ryder stepped into the hallway, pulled the bedroom door nearly closed behind him, and answered. “You’re up late for an old man.”

The voice on the other end was not his brother’s. It was a woman’s, low and shaking with the effort of holding steady, and Ryder recognized it before she said his name. His brother’s mate had never called him from Rhett’s phone. She had never called him at all. “Ryder,” she said. “He’s down.”
