Chapter OneThe Stain on the Sky

First, the sky was on fire. The small one ran. He did not know the word for what he was. He only knew the word they screamed down at him between the burning: stain. Stain. Stain. Wings too big for his body. Legs too small for the mountain. The light behind him was gold and the light inside him was blue and both of them wanted him dead.

He fell more than he ran. Then he only fell. At the bottom of the world there was a crack in the rock, and the crack became a cave, and the cave became dark, and the dark did not scream at him. So he stayed.

Vuk smelled the gold before he saw it. Not metal. Not coin. Not treasure. Burnt gold had a smell the ground-born never knew, sulfur and ash and old heat, the breath of high things that had never once needed permission to take up space in the sky. He went still against the black shale. Above him, the crags rose in broken teeth, sharp enough to cut clouds open. Below him, the timberline waited, dark and thick and low. Another mile, perhaps less, and if he reached the trees before dusk, the pines would hide him until the moon forced his body to betray him.

One more day was all he needed. One more day in the shape of a man. He had what he had come for, a name. It weighed nothing, and it was the heaviest thing he carried. Vuk moved again, barefoot over stone sharp enough to bite. Blood slicked the underside of his left foot and left thin red half-moons behind him. His clothes were torn at one shoulder, dark with old weather and wolf-dirt, too thin for the wind that dragged its claws down from the peaks. He did not look up because looking up was how the sky learned your name.

He kept his shoulders rounded. He kept his breath small. He kept to the shadows where the ridge folded inward and the shale broke loose beneath his hands. The moon would rise full tomorrow, and he could already feel it inside his bones. A pressure sat behind the joints, a pull under the skin, the slow, merciless tightening of a body that was never fully his except on the days it hurt the least. When he gripped the shale, a vein in his wrist glowed faint blue through the skin, brightening with every heartbeat. Day twenty-nine. The disguise was wearing thin.

His wolf blood wanted lower ground, trees, dirt, a hollow beneath roots where no wing could reach him. His dragon blood hated the thought of hiding. It curled hot beneath his ribs, insulted by his bent back and bleeding feet, wanting height, air, a roar thrown across the peaks hard enough to crack snow from the summits. Vuk gave both halves nothing because he had survived by refusing them. Then the birds stopped, not one by one but all at once. The silence struck the mountain so completely that his own breath became too loud. Vuk froze. A shadow passed over him.

It swallowed the ridge, the stones, the blood beneath his feet. For one heartbeat the world had no color at all, only darkness, immense and moving, sliding across the crags like night had come down in the shape of wings. He looked up. The dragon sailed between the peaks with the slow, effortless violence of a falling cathedral. Gold. Purebred. Ancient enough that its horns had begun to pale at the tips. Large enough that the air bent beneath it.

Every scale caught the weak sun and threw it back in molten shards. Its wings stretched wider than the ravine below. Its tail dragged through cloud, splitting vapor into ribbons. Vuk pressed himself flatter against the shale, but he was already too late. The dragon's head turned. One vast eye, bright as a furnace door, fixed on him. For a moment neither of them moved. Vuk knew what it saw.

Not prey, not truly, because prey belonged somewhere in the order of things. Deer belonged to forest. Fish belonged to river. Wolves belonged to dirt, and dragons belonged to sky. Vuk belonged nowhere. To purebreds, beauty was symmetry and gold. Vuk was an asymmetric sin, wolf where there should have been scale, fur where there should have been armor, blue fire where there should have been red ruin. Even in human skin, they knew something was wrong with him.

The dragon opened its mouth. The sound that came out was not a roar. It was judgment. The ridge shook beneath him. Vuk ran. Stone shattered where he had been crouched, and heat lashed the back of his neck before the fire struck. Dragonfire did not begin with flame; it began by stealing the air. His lungs clenched empty, his throat closed around nothing, and then the fire hit the shale behind him and the mountain exploded.

He threw himself sideways as shards of black stone ripped past his face. One sliced his cheek open, while another buried itself in his thigh. He did not stop. He could not stop. The purebred dropped. It did not fly so much as fall, folding its wings and coming down the mountain like the sky had decided to collapse. Vuk lunged over the ridge and slid down a vertical scree field on his back. The world became stone and blood.

Shale tore through his shirt, opened his shoulders, and scraped skin from his spine. He drove his heels down, trying to slow himself, but the loose rock carried him faster. Above him, gold wings snapped open with a crack that rolled over the peaks. Heat bloomed again. Vuk twisted onto his stomach and dug his fingers into the sliding stones. His nails split, and his palms opened. The fire passed over him so close that the tips of his hair burned away and the wet blood on his back hissed. His dragon blood screamed.

Turn. Rise. Burn. His wolf blood screamed louder. Down. Hide. Live. He hit the bottom of the slope hard enough to drive the breath from his body and kept moving on hands and knees until his legs remembered their work. The timberline was closer now. He could see the dark pines gathered below like a closed mouth. If he reached the trees, he might live.

If he shifted, he might fly. If he shifted, he would burn blue. Every dragon in the high clan would see him. The first one would wound him, and the rest would finish the work. Vuk stumbled upright and ran on two human legs while the thing inside him beat itself bloody against his bones. The gold dragon skimmed low over the ridge. Its talons struck the rock behind him and carved trenches deep enough to bury a man standing. The impact threw him forward.

He rolled and hit a boulder with his shoulder, feeling something tear. Pain flashed white behind his eyes. He got up. He always got up. Nobody had ever come back for him when he fell. Not when he was small. Not when the sky first learned what color his fire burned. Not when the dragons decided what he was. So he got up because the alternative had never once arrived.

The ravine opened ahead of him without warning, three thousand feet of empty air. Vuk stopped at the edge so abruptly that loose stones spilled past his toes and vanished into the fog below. There was no bridge, no ledge, and no path down. Behind him, the dragon landed. The mountain bowed beneath its weight. Gold claws gripped the stone, and steam curled from its nostrils. Its head lowered until one burning eye filled Vuk's world. The dragon looked at him the way clean things look at rot.

Then it spoke. "Stain." The word rolled through the rock. Vuk's mouth filled with blood, and he swallowed it. The dragon's jaws opened. Fire gathered in the dark of its throat, red and gold and hungry. Vuk stepped backward off the cliff. For one breath, there was peace. There was only cold air, only falling, only the strange relief of having chosen the impossible before the world chose it for him.

Then his body broke itself open. The shift tore through him in midair. His spine arched until he thought it would snap. White fur ripped through human skin, bone split and lengthened, and his hands clawed into the air as fingers bent, blackened, sharpened. Membrane wings burst from his back with the sound of sails cracking in a storm. Pain blinded him. It always did.

White fur flowed across a body built for the sky. Pale scales gleamed beneath it like frost trapped beneath fresh snow. Great wings unfurled behind him, silver-white and vast, their membranes threaded with veins of living blue fire. Not flame, but starlight. It glowed beneath his skin and spilled through his fur, turning every drifting snowflake around him sapphire and silver. For one impossible heartbeat, he hung between earth and sky. He was a creature neither bloodline could claim.

Beautiful enough to become a legend. Wrong enough to become a warning. Vuk knew only one of those truths. The dragon had taught him which one. He did not become beautiful. He became visible. Blue starlight fire ignited beneath his veins, spilling through white fur and pale scale, bright as a wound in the dusk. The sky around him flashed with it.

Above, the gold dragon screamed. Vuk forced his wings open, and pain answered. The left one caught the wind wrong. The right beat once, hard enough to turn his fall into a wild, crooked plunge. He clipped the cliffside, smashed through a shelf of ice, and spun out over the lower valley. For a heartbeat, he saw everything: the dragon peaks behind him, cruel and high; the wolf forest below, black and waiting; the moon, pale and unfinished in the afternoon sky, watching him before its time. Then gold filled his vision. The purebred struck him from above.

A talon tore through his left wing membrane from bone to trailing edge. Vuk's cry ripped out of him, half howl, half roar, all pain. Blue fire spilled from the wound like glowing ink. It streamed behind him as he fell, bright drops scattering down the mountain, each one hissing where it struck the snow. He tried to beat his wings. The torn one folded. The ground rose. Pines rushed up beneath him.

Vuk did not fall like a dragon. He fell like a stone with wings. He hit the first tree and broke it in half. The second shattered beneath his weight. The third slowed him enough to save his life and punish him for keeping it. He crashed into the deep snow at the edge of the lower valley and carved a trench through white drifts and frozen earth. Blue fire flashed once, then guttered. His torn wing dragged behind him, leaving a luminous smear across the snow.

For a while, there was no sound. Then breath returned, and with it, pain. Vuk lay on his side beneath the broken pines, shaking. The high wind did not reach this low. The valley smelled of sap, snow, wolf, and blood. His blood, too much of it, red where the human skin had returned and blue where the dragon would not let go. He tried to rise. His arm buckled.

The shift abandoned him in pieces. White fur sank beneath torn flesh. Scale withdrew like shame. The ruined wing shuddered once and folded back into a body too small to hold that much hurt. He became a man again because the beast no longer had strength enough to remain. He was naked, bleeding, half-buried in snow, and alive. Above the peaks, the gold dragon circled once. It did not descend.

The purebreds hated the dirt. They hated the trees, the roots, the smell of living things pressed close together. They would burn a mountain clean, but they would not crawl beneath its branches. Vuk laughed once. It came out broken. The sound pulled blood up his throat. He turned his face into the snow and tried to breathe through it. Then he smelled them.

Wolves. Not one, but many. His eyes opened, and shapes moved between the trees. Dark bodies slipped through the pines while yellow eyes caught the last of the light. They circled wide at first, silent as falling ash, their paws making no sound over the snow. Vuk tried to pull his arm beneath him. He could not. The wolves came closer.

One stepped over the broken trunk of a pine and stopped. Another lowered its head. A third bared its throat. Vuk saw none of that. He saw teeth. He saw hunger. He saw the valley learning what the sky already knew. Something like him did not belong anywhere.

The wolves formed a ring around the place where he bled. They did not attack. That was worse. They watched him. The dragons had driven him from the sky. Now the wolves had come to see what had fallen into the dirt. Vuk closed his eyes. Snow melted beneath his cheek.

Blood warmed the hollow around his ribs. Somewhere above him, beyond the broken branches and the smoke of his own body, the moon waited for tomorrow night. He had one thought before the dark took him. It was not fear, and it was not hope. It was only the old, tired certainty that had followed him from every height to every hollow of his life. Even here. Even here. The dark reached for him.

It almost had him when the ring broke. Paws moved, not closer but away, and then one set came closer. Something heavy dropped into the snow beside him. The drift shuddered beneath his cheek. The smell reached him last: blood, hot, fresh, opened, and not his. He could not lift his head. He could not make the thought. The wolves were gone before the question formed, and the dark took him with it still unasked.

Chapter TwoThe Self-Contained Machine

The transition from dying to living was always a matter of engineering. Vuk did not wake up all at once. He woke up by inches, starting with the cold in his fingernails and ending with the weight of the fresh venison flank sitting three inches from his nose. The wolves were long gone. The snow around the carcass had frozen into a hard, dark crust, catching the blue-white light of a dawn that didn't care who had survived the night. He didn't touch the meat.

He had decided what such things meant a long time ago. An exchange he had never agreed to: their silence for his hunger. Stay up there. Eat this. Do not descend. It was the cleanest reading, and it was the one that cost him the least, because a tax was a thing you could refuse and a kindness was a thing that would eventually want something back. The wolves left him the first cut of every hunt, the best of the kill, the heart and the marrow-heavy haunch, and laid it on the snow and ran from it as if the snow itself might bite. They did not do that for a neighbor. They did that for a thing you appeased. He understood appeasement. Appeasement he could survive.

The flank steamed faintly in the cold, fresh, generous, the cut made by a careful hand. He looked at it the way a man looks at a debt collector's gift. It did not occur to him that there was another way to read it. That the best of the kill might be the only language a frightened people had for love, or that running from the snow might be reverence and not disgust. It had stopped occurring to him before his beard came in. The hunger gnawed at him, two days deep now, and he set it beside all the other things he did not let himself have, in the long cold room where he kept them. Vuk dug his human fingers into the red earth beneath the drift, gritted his teeth until a molar clicked, and began the two-mile crawl back up to the mouth of the shale.

The cave did not have a door, but it had a logic. A mile above the timberline, where the wind grew teeth, the granite split into three narrow chambers, and over a hundred years he had given each of them a single use and never let the uses mix. The first was for the weather. It caught the snow and the dead leaves and the worst of the wind, filtering the mountain's anger until the air that reached the inner rooms was thin and dry and still. Nothing lived in the first chamber. It was a lung, and it did its breathing so the rest of the cave didn't have to.

The second was for the body. It held the hearth, a shallow depression lined with river stones he had carried up one at a time across a century, each stone chosen for how it held heat and set back in the exact place its predecessor had cracked. It held the bed of cured hides, the niche of tools ordered by length, the jars, the twine, the grease. Everything a body needed to keep running was in the second chamber, and nothing a body wanted was, because wanting was not a function he maintained. The third chamber was for the dark.

Vuk dragged his naked, bleeding legs across the threshold of the second room. The shift had left him in rags of his own skin, the way it always did when the change came under duress instead of under the slow pull of a clean moon. His left shoulder was a black-and-purple ruin where the purebred's talon had sheared through his wing membrane at the joint. In human form, it translated to a jagged, five-inch trench across his scapula, the lips of it gaping and slow, leaking a fluid that was too thick to be human blood and too blue to be safe. Where it touched the snow on his skin it hissed, faint and steady, the cold light fighting the cold water and losing by inches.

He did not groan. Groaning filled a room with things you couldn't clean up afterward, and he had learned young that the cave kept whatever you put into it. Better to put nothing in. Better to make the sound of a man who feels something, only inside, where it stayed level and did not echo. He reached into a niche in the rock, pulled down a jar of rendered bear grease and a length of coarse hemp twine, and sat down with his back against the freezing stone. He didn't use fire. Not today.

On day thirty, the heat inside him was a volatile thing, a match held over dry grass; the smallest indulgence and it would not stay small. If he let his dragon blood bank high enough to dull the pain, the blue light would leak through the cracks in the cave mouth and stand against the snow like a struck flint. The dragons would read it from the peaks the way they had read it the night before. The wolves would read it from the valley floor and add a verse. Every creature that wanted him dead or holy navigated by that light, and the only safety he had ever found was in keeping it banked beneath the waterline of his own cold. So he stayed cold. He had been staying cold for a hundred years. He was very good at it.

He hooked his right foot under a shelf of solid granite, wrapped both hands around his left wrist, set his jaw, and pulled. The relocation came in two stages, the way it always did with this shoulder: a long grinding travel where the head of the bone dragged across the socket's lip, and then the pop, a wet, flat sound that echoed off the low ceiling and came back smaller. The pain whited everything out. Vuk closed his eyes, his forehead dropping to his knees, and counted his own heartbeat until the gray spots in his vision thinned and broke apart. Three minutes. He had learned the exact count a long time ago. The body did the same things in the same order every time; you only had to wait it out and not waste anything while you waited. When the world stopped spinning, he began to sew.

The needle was a sliver of rib-bone he had sharpened against the river stones until it would pass through hide without tearing. He threaded it with the hemp, pinched the lips of the trench together with two fingers, and drove it through. The first pass was always the worst, the skin resisting and then giving all at once. He worked from the top of the wound down, the way he mended everything, each stitch the same width from the last, pulling the twine snug but not cruel, knotting at intervals so that a single tear would not unravel the whole line. He passed the bone through his own meat with the steady, unblinking precision of a cobbler mending a boot. He did not feel the skin tear, not really; the feeling reached him from a long way off, reporting in, and he noted it and set it down and made the next stitch. He had spent a century learning to treat his own body like a piece of equipment that required maintenance. You oiled the hinges. You patched the canvas. You checked the seams against the next hard use, and you did not ask the machine how it felt about the work, because the machine did not have anyone to tell.

By the time the sun reached its noon height, the shoulder was bound, the grease had sealed the blue fluid beneath a layer of lard, and he was dressed in a clean shirt of coarse gray wool. He looked normal. He looked like a man who might live in a village, who might have a brother, who might have been asked by someone if the winter had been kind to his crops. But as he gripped the edge of his tunic, the veins in his wrist pulsed, a faint, starlight blue glowing visibly beneath the pale skin, brightening with every heavy heartbeat. Day thirty. The disguise was straining against his bones. The order of the room was perfect. The firewood was stacked by length. The water stone was full. It was a century of maintenance, and it felt exactly like peace, provided he never tried to breathe too deeply.

In the third chamber, the darkness was absolute. Vuk did not need a lamp; his eyes adjusted until the faint, phosphorescent veins in the granite looked like tangled roots. There was nothing in this room. No furs, no tools, no firewood stacked by length. He had kept it empty on purpose, because it was not for the body. It was the one chamber he came to in order to remember, and remembering needed room. He sat down on the cold stone, his bones aching with the weight of thirty days of holding his breath, and he began the roll call the way he began it after every moon: out loud, in the dark, to no one.

The three pines by the eastern ridge. Fallen to lightning, winter of ninety-four. The gray bitch with the torn ear. Died of the throat-cough behind the beaver dam. The spring that tasted of iron below the red crag. Dry since the midsummer drought. The bull elk with the splintered antler that wintered three years in the high meadow. Taken by the pack in the autumn drive. He did not say the names to cast spells. He said them because the world outside this cave was constantly erasing itself. The forest forgot its dead; the snow covered the tracks; the wolves changed Alphas and changed the songs, burying old names under new choruses. If he did not hold them in his mouth, the proof that they had ever occupied space beneath the sun would vanish into the dirt, and a thing that no one remembered was a thing that had never been. He was the only rememberer this mountain had. It was poor work and endless and he had appointed himself to it because the alternative was unbearable.

He added the new one to the count, the thing he had carried down the mountain, the thing the dragon had nearly burned out of the world a day early. The stone stair above the third ravine. Swallowed by the ice this winter. It was a road once. No one living remembers where it went. It had weighed nothing. It had nearly cost him the sky and both his legs. It was held now. The mountain could keep the rest. Then, the way he always ended, he reached for the oldest one. The first thing he had ever taught himself to keep, before he understood that keeping was all he would ever be allowed to do.

Anka. A wolf the color of bankside willow. The heat of her over him in the dark while the sky came apart with wings and fire. The last thing between a hunted child and the things that hunt children, and then not there anymore, and the cold rushing into the place she had been. He could hold her that far. He had never been able to hold what came after, or the face of the one who should have stood beside her and never knew there was anything to stand beside. There the memory simply stopped, like a road swallowed by ice. No one living remembered where it went. He was not certain he did either. He sat with the silence where the rest of her should have been for a long time, the blue light in his veins pulsing once against the dark, casting a long, crooked shadow of his hand against the cave wall. Then he let her go, the way he let her go every month.

The silence of the cave settled back over him, heavy and familiar as the stone itself. Outside, the first flakes of an afternoon storm had begun to fall, and the wind worked at them until the light going gray was the only way to tell the hour was changing. The storm blew itself out by dusk. Vuk stood at the cave mouth and watched the clouds come apart over the valley, and he made his accounting the way he made it every month. He had maintained the perimeter. He had fixed the shoulder. He had refused the meat. The machine was clean, balanced, and perfectly empty. He was safe. He was sovereign. He was entirely within his own borders. He had one hour left to believe it.

Tonight the moon would be round. Tonight his body would break him open and fling him into the sky to circle the light like a moth around a lantern, while the things below sang to his shadow. It did not ask. It had never once asked, not the first time when he was a boy and did not understand what was happening to him, not the four hundredth, not tonight. The dread of it was not sharp anymore. It was the dull, familiar dread of a man who knows that at a fixed hour a door he cannot lock will open, and that he will walk through it, and that he will not remember clearly what he does on the other side, only that the wind will be very cold and the light will pull and pull and the singing will rise to meet him as if he had answered a question no one had the courage to ask out loud. He could feel it gathering already, low in the spine, the heat coming up off the waterline he kept it under. The veins in his wrists were bright enough now to throw shadow.

He walked out onto the shelf of rock above the cave, because a body that size could not be born indoors. The snow was already filling his footprints behind him, erasing the path of a man walking toward a thing he could not refuse. The moon came up over the eastern teeth of the mountains, white and whole. The first pull came at the base of his spine. He did not fight it. Fighting only moved the breaking somewhere worse. The shift took the sewn shoulder first. He felt the first stitch tear. And far below, in the dark between the pines, the wolves began to sing.
